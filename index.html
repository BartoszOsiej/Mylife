A Word from the Author

This book was written in silence. In that same silence that is its main theme. I wrote it in the evenings, at night, in moments when the world went quiet and I was left alone with my thoughts. It's not a book about great events — it's about the small moments that make up our lives. About the silence between words, about the stillness between people, about the truth hiding behind our masks.

I don't pretend to be a philosopher or a sage. I'm an ordinary man trying to understand the world — and himself. This book is my way of sharing what I saw when I stopped pretending and started looking. I don't claim that what I see is the ultimate truth — because such a thing doesn't exist. But it is the truth as I saw it. And maybe someone else will see a piece of themselves in it.

If so — the goal has been reached.

Bartosz Osiej

LIFE – Volume I: Shadows of True Living

Author: Bartosz Osiej

FOREWORD

SILENCE AND THE TRUE SELF

I never intended to write about how nice everything is. And yet every silence filling my apartment became a teacher that, without a word, showed me the truth about myself, about the world, and about the people I met.

I first understood the power of silence when I was maybe eight years old. We sat at the table — my father, my mother, me. Dinner had been going on for two hours. Nobody said a word. My father ate slowly, thoughtfully, as if every vegetable on the plate demanded its own decision. My mother sipped tea, staring out the window where dusk was falling. And I sat between them and felt something I couldn't name back then — a dense, almost tangible layer of unspoken words hanging over the table like steam over hot soup.

They didn't fight. They didn't shout. They didn't throw plates. They just went quiet — each in their own way, each in their own corner of silence. And as a child, I learned to read those silences like code. By how my father set down his fork — loudly or softly — I knew what kind of evening awaited us. By whether my mother poured herself a second or third cup of tea, I could predict if she'd go to bed in front of the TV or stay in the kitchen writing in her notebook.

Every second alone was like a mirror reflecting the smallest tremor of an eyelid, a quiet breath, a silent sigh — everything that could betray what I truly felt, truly thought, truly wanted. Watching the world through the window, I noticed thousands of small signs that daylight would wash away but silence brought into sharp focus. I learned to analyze every movement, every glance, every gesture of passersby — the way they folded their hands on a table, the way they fell silent in response to questions that were never asked.

I remember the neighbor from the third floor — Mrs. Jadwiga. A woman past seventy, gaunt, always in the same gray sweater. For years I passed her on the stairwell and we exchanged maybe ten words: "Good morning," "How are you feeling?" "Fine, thank you." The standard repertoire. But one evening, coming home late, I found her sitting on the stairs — the same stairs she climbed every day with difficulty, holding the railing. She sat in the dark and cried. Quietly, soundlessly, face buried in her hands. I didn't know what to do. I stood a few steps above and waited. After a moment she raised her head, saw me, and said: "I'm sorry you saw this. It's just — today is the anniversary of my husband's death. Thirty years, and I still don't know what to do in the evening when no one tells me to go to sleep."

In those silences I discovered the truth about their intentions, their emotions, their fears and desires. Because people say many words — but it's the silence that truly betrays who they are.

I discovered that truth never comes immediately. It comes slowly, subtly, in the form of small signals whose meaning must be decoded. Every shadow in the room, every ray of sun reflecting off a wall, every sound of the city in the background was part of that map — teaching me that a man who can't listen to his own interior will never see the truth in the world outside.

I saw it with my colleagues at work. Guys I drank beer with after shifts, who laughed at my jokes and slapped me on the back. They seemed genuine — until the company started laying people off. Then the same one who yesterday said "we're like family" wouldn't look me in the eye in the hallway. Because he knew his position depended on how many of us remained. And he'd rather be with those who stay than with those who leave. I don't blame him. But I remembered that moment — the moment the facade popped like a soap bubble.

I understood that life gives us no instruction manual. Every step, every decision is an attempt to find meaning that isn't obvious. Silence let me hear it, see it, feel it — even when it meant admitting my own weakness, uncertainty, and the pain that can't be avoided.

In that silence I learned to observe people as never before. It wasn't about small finger movements or trembling eyelids — though those too became a legible language for me. It was about something deeper: the ability to catch the moments when a person stops pretending. When the mask falls — not because of some dramatic event, but because of plain exhaustion.

One day on the bus, a man in a suit sat next to me. Maybe forty, sharply groomed, briefcase beside him. He looked like someone who gets up in the morning, shaves, puts on a tie, and goes to earn money. But when the bus stopped at the station and the afternoon sun poured through the window, I saw his face change. His shoulders dropped, his breathing slowed, and in his eyes appeared an expression that can't be faked — exhaustion so deep it was physical. As if for one second he forgot he was wearing a mask. And then, before I could think anything, it was over — again the same suited man, straight back, determined gaze. That one second told me more about him than a year of daily "hey, how's it going?"

Those moments taught me that people are never what they seem at first glance, and that knowing someone truly requires patience and the courage to look past the surface.

Silence was the mirror reflecting my whole life — my mistakes, my fears, my successes, my desires, all the masks I wore to survive in a world that didn't ask who I was, only demanded that I fit its rhythm. In that mirror I saw myself completely honestly, full of contrasts and hidden harmony forming the whole of my personality.

And then the writing began. Not because I had something to say — rather because the silence finally became so loud that I had to put it somewhere. Words poured out of me like water from a burst pipeline — uneven, chaotic, but with such pressure that it couldn't be stopped. I'd sit at my desk in the evenings, turn on the lamp, and write. Sometimes until three in the morning. Sometimes just two pages. Sometimes twenty. But every time I felt the same thing — as if I were drawing out of myself something I'd carried inside for years, something I couldn't name any other way.

Truth doesn't come in simple forms. It demands observation, introspection, patience, and the courage to accept the consequences of knowing yourself. And when I looked at myself without mercy and without illusion — then, for the first time, I felt I could write something that matters not just to me. That maybe someone else, reading these words, will feel what I felt on those stairs beside the weeping Mrs. Jadwiga — that they are not alone in their silence.

THE ILLUSION OF COMFORT

Every person craves safety. It seems attainable through money, relationships, routine, social status — anything that gives the illusion of control over life. But those foundations are fragile and can collapse in an instant, revealing the illusion that for years gave false comfort.

The people around me reacted to every signal — analyzing every glance, every word, to assess how much I could offer them. Their value in their own eyes rose or fell at a speed that can't be measured. In those moments, true faces surfaced — revealing who is beside me for me, and who only for what I can give.

I remember a party at a friend's place — a garden barbecue, beer in hand, laughter. Everyone was smiling, everyone was talking, everyone felt part of something. And then one person said: "Listen, Tomek's broke." And suddenly the music changed. Not literally — the silence between the notes changed. People started glancing at each other, at their glasses, at the lawn. No one said anything bad — but no one said anything good either. Those who an hour earlier told Tomek how wonderful he was now suddenly had urgent calls to take at home. Comfort vanished — and with it, everyone who was anchored in it.

People are masters of hiding their true intentions. You don't need X-ray glasses to see it — just attention. Just noticing how someone suddenly changes the subject when the conversation brushes against their real motivations. How their voice gets quieter when I ask about things that hurt. How they look away when I say something that demands an authentic reaction instead of a rehearsed phrase.

One of my bosses — a smart guy, a great head for business — used to say: "In a company there are no friends, only coworkers." He said it with such conviction, as if he'd discovered some fundamental truth about the universe. And for a long time I believed he was right. But over time I understood: that's not a truth about companies — it's a truth about him. Because people who can't build relationships on anything but gain project their own helplessness onto the whole world. And the world is bigger than their fear.

In time I understood that real relationships reveal themselves in moments of crisis, when there's nothing left but yourself. Only then can you see who stays, who leaves, and who waits for everything to return to comfort so they can return with a fake smile and borrowed concern. In such moments every presence has value, and silence becomes a tool that exposes the truth.

But there's something worse than losing comfort — realizing comfort never existed. That what we called "stability" was only the habit of repeating the same days. That what we called "friendship" was only mutual convenience. And that what we called "life" was only a permanent refusal to live — because avoiding pain is not the same as living.

PREPARING FOR THE JOURNEY INTO SHADOW

Life is a journey through shadow, where everything is less clear, more raw, more demanding of attention. Comfort is an illusion that vanishes the moment real weight appears in existence. Every step, every decision demands full presence and courage.

Silence becomes a guide that lets you see what remains invisible in the noise of the day. The things that define the value of relationships, of people, of the decisions we make every day. Observing every movement, every shadow in an eye, every pause in a conversation, I learn to read people like a book whose pages are hidden from those who can't look carefully.

When I started writing this book, one thought was in my head: "People don't tell the truth. Not even to themselves." Not out of malice — out of habit. From childhood we learn to say what others expect of us. "How are you?" — "Fine." "What's up?" — "Okay." "Everything alright?" — "Yeah." Those three words — fine, okay, yeah — make up the entire repertoire of our communication. And behind them hide whole stories — stress, loneliness, despair, longing, a silence that boils like water in a pot with the lid on.

I wanted to write a book about what hides behind those three words. About the truth that doesn't fit into a polite "fine." About emotions that have no name in the language we use every day. About the shadows we cast on ourselves and others — not because we want to, but because we don't know how else to be.

And I know this book — this journey — won't be easy. Because life's real lessons don't come in the form we expect. They come when everything else fails. When we're left alone with our choices and their consequences. When shadow becomes the only companion, and the only light is the one we carry inside.

I don't promise that after reading this book it'll be lighter. I don't promise you'll find answers to the questions that torment you. But I promise one thing: you won't be alone. Because every story I tell here is the story of someone who — like you — walked through shadow and searched for light. Sometimes they found it. Sometimes not. But they always kept walking.

This is a book about shadows. But shadows exist only where there's light. So somewhere between one and the other — between loss and rebirth, between betrayal and forgiveness, between silence and the word — there's something we might call life. And that's what this book is about.

THREE TRUTHS ABOUT LIFE

If I had to condense this entire book into three sentences, they'd read like this:

First: life is not fair. Not because the world is evil — but because the world has no obligation to be fair. It is what it is. And our task isn't to complain about what it is, but to learn to live in it — with dignity, with courage, with hope.

Second: people are not perfect. No one is. Even those we love. Even those who love us. And that imperfection — that human, painful, irritating imperfection — is part of the beauty. Because without it there'd be no forgiveness. And without forgiveness there'd be no closeness.

Third: silence is worth more than words. Because in silence, truth is born — the real, uncensored, raw truth that words can't express. And that truth — however painful — is the only thing that sets us free.

These three truths aren't original. They aren't new. They aren't surprising. But they are true — at least for me. And maybe someone else, reading them, will think: "Yeah, I feel that too." And then these words — these simple, ordinary, imperfect words — will make sense. Because the meaning of words isn't their novelty. It's that someone understands them.

AN ALPHABET OF GRATITUDE

If I had to write a letter of gratitude using the alphabet, it would look like this:

A is for Attention — for the times someone actually listened. B is for Bread — hot from the oven, smelling like home. C is for Silence — which taught me the truth. D is for Dad — and the apple tree in whose shade we sat.

E is for Emotion — for existing, though it hurt. F is for Failure — which taught me to seek the truth. G is for Goodbye — which taught me not to live in the past. H is for Harmony — the music that kept me company in silence.

I is for I — for the simple fact that I exist. J is for That One Second — the moment everything changed. K is for "I love you" — the words that finally came. L is for Lamp — the light in a dark room.

M is for Marek — for the phone call when I had no one to call. N is for No — the boundary I learned to set. O is for Father — the silence that said more than words. P is for Pardon — the freedom it brings.

Q is for Quiet — the moments I still remember. R is for Resistance — the strength it takes. S is for Time — for the patience with which it teaches. T is for Truth — for the lessons that can't be learned any other way.

U is for Understanding — for the courage to choose myself. V is for Vulnerability — the bridge it builds between people.

And this alphabet — this incomplete, imperfect alphabet of gratitude — is my way of saying: thank you. For everything. For nothing. For all that was, is, and will be.

THE KILOGRAMS OF TRUTH

Truth has weight. Not physical — emotional. The more truth you carry inside you, the harder it is to walk. And the longer you hide it, the heavier it grows — like snow on a roof that piles up and up until the roof can't hold and collapses.

I carried such a truth for years. It wasn't a great truth — no betrayal, no lie, no crime. It was a truth about me — about who I really am when no one is watching. About my fears, my weaknesses, my secrets. And that truth — that quiet, hidden truth, carried like a stone in my pocket — weighed more than anything else in my life.

When I finally said it — to someone I trusted — I felt such relief that I wept. Not from pain — from freedom. Because when you speak the truth, you lift a weight off yourself. And suddenly it turns out you can keep going. You can breathe. You can live — without hiding, without pretending, without wearing a mask.

And that lesson — the lesson that truth is lighter than a lie — is perhaps the most important lesson of my life. Because a lie is heavy. Hiding is heavy. Pretending is heavy. And truth — however painful — is light. So light it's hard to believe that something like that was ever heavy.

A LETTER TO MYSELF FROM TEN YEARS AGO

If I could write a letter to myself from ten years ago, it would read like this:

"Hey. I know you think you know everything right now. That you know the world, know people, know yourself. You don't. And that's okay. Because nobody does. And nobody has to.

I want to tell you one thing: stop being afraid. Stop fearing loss. Stop fearing pain. Stop fearing loneliness. Because all of it — every pain, every loss, every moment of solitude — is part of the road. And that road, however winding and uneven, will take you where you need to go.

I'm not saying it'll be easy. It'll be hard. There will be nights you can't sleep. There will be days you want to give up. There will be moments you think it isn't worth it. But it is. It's always worth it. Because this life — this imperfect, painful, error-filled life — is the only one you have. And there's no point wasting it on fear.

And remember one thing: you are not alone. There are people who love you — even if you can't see it. There are people who think of you — even if they don't say it. And there's someone — me, your older self — who looks at you with pride. Not because you achieved great things. But because you survived. And that's the greatest success a person can achieve: to survive and remain yourself."

THE MEMORY OF WATER

There's a smell I associate with home — not just any home, but the first one, from childhood. The smell of water mixed with soap and orange. That smell stayed with me for years before I understood where it came from.

My mother always cleaned on Saturdays. Not because she had to — she just did. She'd get up in the morning, turn on the radio — always the same radio, always the same station — and start cleaning. First the kitchen, then the living room, then the bathroom. And I, as a child, lying on the couch listening to those sounds — the hum of the vacuum, the click of doors, the whisper of the radio — felt safe.

Because those sounds were constant. Unchanging. The same every day. And that constancy — that predictability — was an anchor holding me to the ground while the world around was full of things I didn't understand.

Only years later, when I started cleaning my own apartment, did I understand where the smell came from. It wasn't the smell of soap or orange. It was the smell of a mother's presence — the smell of someone who cares for you even when you don't see it. Even when you're behind your door, in your room, in your world. Even when you don't say "thank you" — because you don't know there's anything to thank for.

And that smell — that invisible, unspoken, ungraspable smell — is part of me. Just like my father's silence, like my mother's singing, like the quiet of the house I grew up in. Because home isn't walls. Home is the smells, sounds, and silences we carry inside us even when we're thousands of kilometers from where we were born.

AN EVENING WITH A BOOK

There's one evening each year I always associate with reading. It's the first evening you come home and it's already dark. Not the winter dark — that one is brutal, short, unpleasant. But the autumn-winter dark — the one that falls early yet is warm and soft, as if the night wants to wrap around you, not frighten you.

On such evenings I sit in my chair, turn on the lamp, pick up a book, and read. Not because I have to — because I want to. Because reading is the only activity where you can be in two worlds at once — the one you live in and the one you create in your imagination.

And those two worlds — the real and the imagined — meet in my head. They mix, interpenetrate, enrich each other. Because the world of the book teaches me something about the real world — about people, about emotions, about situations I never experienced but understand, because someone described them so truly that I feel them as my own.

I remember a book that changed my life — I won't say its title, because this isn't an ad. But I remember reading it: at night, by lamplight, in silence, and suddenly feeling that someone was writing about me. That those words, those sentences, those stories — were mine. That the author, whom I never met, knows me better than my closest people.

And then I understood why people write. Not for money, not for fame, not for recognition. They write to find someone who will understand them. Who will read their words and say: "I feel that too." And that shared silence — that silence between author and reader — is perhaps the most beautiful bridge literature can build.

SAYING GOODBYE TO YOUTH

Youth ends before you notice it. You're sitting in a café, drinking coffee, watching people, and suddenly you realize you're not young anymore. Not physically — spiritually. Because youth isn't an age. Youth is the belief that everything is ahead of you. That time is unlimited. That mistakes can be fixed. That tomorrow will always be better than today.

I remember the moment I realized my youth was over. I stood at a bus stop — maybe thirty years old — watching a group of teenagers. They were laughing, shoving each other, shouting across the street. They had something I'd lost: lightness. The lightness of being, of existing, of looking at the world as a place full of possibilities rather than threats.

And then I thought: "When did I lose that? When did I stop seeing the world as a place full of possibilities? When did I start seeing it as a place full of threats?" And I couldn't remember. I don't remember the day, the moment, the decision. It happened gradually — like leaves changing color in autumn. Not overnight. Week by week. Year by year. Until one day you look in the mirror and see someone you didn't recognize.

Saying goodbye to youth isn't dramatic. There are no tears, no farewells, no grand declarations. There's just a moment — quiet, subtle, almost invisible — when you understand that something has ended. And something new is beginning. Not better, not worse — different. And that difference — that change, that transition — is part of life. A part you can't avoid, but can accept.

WHY I WRITE

When I started writing this book, I didn't know why I was doing it. I had no plan, no strategy, not even the certainty that anyone would read it. I wrote because I couldn't not write — because the words churned inside me so long that I finally had to release them, the way a man exhales after holding his breath too long.

Writing is a solitary act. You sit at your desk, it's quiet, and you stare at a blank screen. And the blank screen stares back — patient, silent, demanding. And then you begin to write — not because you have something to say, but because you have something to understand. Because writing is a form of thinking — a more honest form than talking to anyone, because there's no second person here to judge you, correct you, redirect you onto other tracks. It's only you and your words — and the truth flowing out of you, even if you don't want to see it.

I remember the first evening I sat down to write. It was late, maybe two in the morning. The city outside the window was silent. I turned on the lamp, opened a document, and started to write. And before I knew it, three hours had passed. I didn't write anything great — a few paragraphs, a few observations, a few memories. But something inside me changed. As if someone opened a window in a stuffy room — suddenly there was more air, more light, more room to breathe.

I wrote regularly from that evening on. Not always well, not always coherent, not always with pleasure. But always honestly. Because I discovered that writing — like silence — is a mirror. It shows you what you hide even from yourself. It forces you to look into places you'd normally avoid. And it gives you something nothing else can: the chance to talk with yourself, without hurry, without judgment, without fear.

This book is the fruit of those evenings — those lonely hours at a desk, in silence, with a lamp and a blank screen. It's a record of what I saw when I stopped pretending and started looking. I don't claim that what I saw is ultimate truth — because ultimate truth doesn't exist. But it's the truth as I saw it. And maybe — somewhere, sometime — someone else will see a piece of themselves in it.

CHAPTER I — THE MIRAGE OF OWNERSHIP

THE ILLUSION OF OWNERSHIP

Don't fool yourself into thinking you can have everything you dream of. The world never gives anything for free, and life can be merciless — even when it first seems gracious.

When I was younger, I believed money guaranteed safety. That it would secure the loyalty of the people I met. That a wallet full of bills would protect me from loneliness and betrayal. But reality soon taught me it was all an illusion — a mist hanging over hard ground, blown away by the first strong wind of truth.

My first serious job was a transport company. I was twenty-three and believed that if I only worked hard enough, the world would repay me with interest. I came first, left last. I took extra shifts. I didn't complain. And when I got my first bonus — five hundred zloty, more than anyone on the team — I felt like a king. I spent it that same evening: dinner at a restaurant I'd normally never choose, a taxi instead of the bus, a new shirt I didn't need.

At the restaurant I met three coworkers — the same ones who the day before said the company didn't appreciate our effort. They sat at a table, drinking expensive wine and laughing. When they saw me, they invited me over. "Bartek! Sit down! You're buying tonight?" — they joked. And I bought. Because I wanted them to know I was doing fine. That I was one of them. That I deserved their respect.

The next day at work it turned out the same people — the ones whose bill I'd paid — were gossiping behind my back about how I was "flaunting my bonus." One of them said: "He thinks just because he has five hundred, he's somebody." And then something inside me broke. Not from anger — from understanding. The understanding that the money I spent on dinner bought me nothing but an hour of fake friendship and weeks of gossip.

My first material successes surrounded me with people whose presence seemed natural and sincere. Yet in moments of trial, most of them turned out to be near me only for gain. For convenience, for a slice of my strength and resources — not for me myself. In time I learned to recognize the signals: the moment someone's smile gets just a little too wide when I talk about my plans. The moment eyes light up at the word "money" and dim when I talk about something that brings no direct benefit.

Life showed me that the illusion of ownership is the hardest trap to recognize. Because it reveals its effects only when everything seems stable. When the people around you show their true faces, motivations, hidden desires. Real wealth turned out to be something entirely different than I thought — not in possession, but in the ability to understand people and recognize who survives the test of time.

There's one more thing I learned about ownership — and it may be the hardest of all. Owning things changes us. Not just our relationships with others, but our relationship with ourselves. When I have, I'm confident. When I lose, I become someone else — someone I didn't know until I had to. And that second version of me — the one forged in the fire of trial — is more real than the first, the comfortable one, the one from the days when the wallet was full.

THE TEST OF LOYALTY

True loyalty reveals itself in times of lack, when there's nothing left but you and the people around you. Then you see who stays and who leaves. Who is beside you for you, and who for appearances, money, or their own comfort.

I've seen it many times. Friends I'd known for years suddenly became indifferent, waiting for a better moment. While those I considered marginal in my life proved unshakable. The contrast was like a blow — because it forced me to revalue everything I'd assumed was certain.

I remember an evening when Marek called me — a man I knew from work. We weren't close. I knew about him what you know about someone you share an office with: that he has a wife, two kids, that he likes a beer after work and always arrives on time. Nothing more. That evening he lost his job. His voice on the phone was quiet, drained of energy, as if every word cost him enormous effort. He didn't ask for money or help finding a new job. He just said: "I wanted to reach out, because I had no one to call."

We lingered on that sentence. For a long moment neither of us said anything. Then Marek began to talk — not about losing work, but about his life. About how for fifteen years he got up at five in the morning, came home at six in the evening, put the kids to bed, ate dinner with his wife, and went to sleep. And how suddenly, overnight, that rhythm broke — and it turned out there was nothing beyond it. No passion, no hobby, no friends besides work. Only emptiness, and the terror that the emptiness would stay forever.

Loyalty has nothing to do with how long you've known someone or how many parties you shared. Loyalty is what comes out when a person is left alone with their fear — and knows who they can call without being judged or used. Marek called me because he knew I wouldn't answer with advice or pity. That I'd simply listen. And that was enough.

There was also the case of Kasia — a girl I met in university. Kasia wasn't my friend. She was more like an acquaintance from shared classes — someone I exchanged maybe twenty conversations with over two years. When I lost my first serious job and had no money for rent, Kasia called. She didn't ask what happened. She didn't ask how much I needed. She said: "I have a spare room. You can come for the weekend, then we'll figure it out." And when I arrived — with one bag and an empty account — she opened the door, pointed to the room, and said: "The towel is in the bathroom. You can make your own tea."

It was the most beautiful gesture I've ever received. There was no pity in it. No pretense. No expectations. Just an open hand — without words, without declarations, without commentary. And that open hand taught me more about loyalty than fifteen years of parties with "friends."

THE RULES OF OWNERSHIP

From the very beginning of life I learned that owning anything — money, things, relationships — carries invisible rules that decide whether something survives the test of time.

Once I talked to a colleague who suddenly lost all his savings. His eyes were full of panic, his hands trembled, and his tone betrayed a fear I'd never seen in him. He kept saying: "I don't understand… I had everything… everything was under control…"

I looked at him closely and answered: "Control is an illusion. It always was — never a real foundation." I didn't say it with superiority. I said it because I'd once stood in the same place myself — with the same expression, the same chill on my back, the same unanswered question: "Now what?"

People learn the rules of ownership only when they lose it. When comfort disappears and every move of the people around becomes a clear signal — who is beside you for you, and who for your assets. These lessons are brutal but necessary. Because they teach that the rules of ownership aren't written in books, but in people's reactions and in the way the world tests your worth.

I also knew the rules of ownership are subtle. You only need to watch the small stuff: the way someone slides their cup across the table when you talk about your problems. How their tone changes at the smallest signal. These small signs betray the real rules of the game being played around you.

I remember the situation that made it all clear to me. I sat in a café with a friend — Marek, a different Marek than the one from work. Marek was talking about his problem: conflicts with his boss, uncertainty about his job, fear of being fired. I listened carefully, nodded, said "I get it." And then, in the gap between his sentences, I glanced at my phone screen — and saw a transfer notification. A large transfer. Money I'd been waiting months for.

And then Marek said something important — and I didn't hear it. Because I was already thinking about the money. About plans. About what I'd buy, what I'd change, where I'd go. Marek finished his story, and I nodded and said: "Yeah, that's important." But I had no idea what he'd said. Because my attention was already elsewhere — where the money was.

That was the moment I understood that I myself am exactly the same person as those I blamed. That money doesn't just change the people around you — it changes me. And that the rules of ownership apply not only to how others react to my money, but to how I react to it myself.

And so, every day, every meeting, every conversation, I watched the rules of ownership in action. I saw people test boundaries, probe loyalty, measure value and reactions. Owning isn't just material goods — it's knowledge, experience, and the ability to read the world in its full complexity.

THE VALUE OF EMPTINESS

Sometimes value lies not in what you have, but in what you lack. An empty wallet, missing resources, temporary solitude — it's in those moments you learn most. Because then every detail becomes visible, every decision matters, and every person around you reveals their true face.

Once, when I'd lost most of my savings, I looked at the people who had seemed like friends. And I watched them go quiet, change their tone. In some eyes appeared a subtle flicker of calculation — as if in an instant they were computing how much they could still squeeze out of me before it paid off to leave.

There was a moment — maybe silly, maybe banal, but I remember it forever. I sat at home, empty, because I'd sold the furniture, eating soup from a pot because I had no bowls. Not because I couldn't afford bowls — I just didn't have the strength to go to the store. And then the doorbell rang. It was the neighbor from the ground floor — Mr. Zbyszek, an older man I'd exchanged maybe five words with in two years. He stood in the doorway with a piece of cake and said: "I saw you weren't home, and you have an empty kitchen. So eat — the cake will go to waste."

That cake — simple, crumbly, with apples — was worth more than all the dinners at expensive restaurants I'd bought for "friends." Because there was no calculation in it. No expectation of return. Not a shadow of transaction. Just human kindness — the most ordinary, the most simple kind, the kind that needs no words to exist.

But there were others too. People I barely knew — the cashier at the store where I always bought coffee, the neighbor from the third floor I'd exchanged maybe ten words with in two years. They stayed. Not because I'd promised them anything. Not because they expected something back. They just stayed — because that's who they were.

The value of emptiness reveals the truth — about people, about the world, about what we really hold inside ourselves. Emptiness is the key that opens your eyes to a reality comfort will never reveal.

Emptiness became my teacher. Every experience of lack, every loss, every moment of solitude taught me patience, observation, and the ability to read true intentions. I learned that the value of owning isn't quantity, but the ability to understand people and the mechanisms that decide what truly remains in your life when everything else disappears.

And there's something paradoxical about emptiness — something you can't understand until you've experienced it. Emptiness isn't the end. It's the beginning. It's the moment you stop pretending you're in control and start building from zero — from what is truly yours, not from what you borrowed from the world.

THE PRICE OF SUCCESS

Success has a price — and that price is always hidden in the fine print nobody reads until it's time to pay.

When I first earned serious money — really serious, the kind that changes how you live — I felt euphoria. The first week I woke up smiling. The second I started planning. The third I noticed my friends starting to change — or I started seeing them differently. I couldn't say which came first.

Success isolates. Not because you want to isolate yourself — but because people around you don't know how to react. Those who were with you in poverty feel threatened by your success. Those who were never close suddenly want to be your best friends. And you stand in the middle, not knowing who to trust, because everyone has something to gain — or something to lose.

One day I sat in my empty apartment — the same one where I once lived with my family — and realized I had no one to call to say I'd succeeded. Because the people who'd be genuinely proud of me were far away. And those who were close — weren't sincere. And suddenly success, which had seemed like the peak of everything, turned out to be a lonely mountain where the wind blows so hard you can't hear your own thoughts.

I learned then that success not shared is like eating in a locked room — it fills you, but it doesn't satisfy. Because human joy doesn't come from having — it comes from sharing. And if you have no one to share with, even the greatest achievement becomes just a number in a bank account — a digit that grows but holds none of the warmth of human touch.

THE TRAP OF COMPARISON

Comparing yourself to others is like looking into a crooked mirror — it always distorts the image. Every time you compare your life to someone else's, you lose sight of what you actually have.

I once had a friend — Maciej — who seemed to have everything. A beautiful wife, a big apartment, a car, regular vacations abroad. Every time I stopped by for coffee I saw all those things and felt a strange tightening in my stomach. Not envy — rather a sense of lack. As if my life were an unfinished painting and his — a finished masterpiece.

And then Maciej called me at three in the morning. His voice was changed — quiet, trembling, as if something had drained everything out of him. "I need to talk to you," he said. We met in a bar that was almost empty. Maciej ordered whiskey, drank it in two gulps, and said: "My wife is cheating on me. And I can't leave her, because I have nowhere to go. Because everything I have is in that apartment. And I'm afraid that if I leave, I'll become no one."

I looked at him and saw a man who on the outside had everything and on the inside — nothing. Who built his life from objects and appearances until it turned out there were no foundations underneath. And then I understood that comparing yourself to others isn't just a trap — it's a lie. Because we never know what hides behind a facade. We never know what what we see on the outside cost.

From that day I stopped comparing. I don't always manage — I'm human, and people compare by nature. But every time I feel that tightening in my stomach, I remember Maciej and his third night in an empty bar. And I come back to myself.

AN ALPHABET OF FEAR

If I had to describe my fear with the alphabet, it would look like this:

A is for Acceptance — the fear I'll never accept myself as I am. B is for Blunder — the fear I'll make a mistake that can't be fixed. C is for Silence — the fear of the silence that forces you to look inward. D is for Daughter — the fear of passing my fears on to my child. E is for Emotion — the fear of emotions drowning me if I let them out.

F is for Falseness — the fear that I'm living a lie and don't realize it. G is for Grief — the fear of words that get stuck in my throat when I should speak them. H is for Humiliation — the fear of a shame that paralyzes. I is for Irrationality — fear of fear itself.

J is for Just One Chance — the fear I'll waste my one chance for change. K is for the Lie — the fear of a lie I repeat so often I start believing it. L is for Leaving — the fear of people who can hurt me. M is for Mutism — the fear of a silence that solves nothing.

N is for the Unknown — the fear of what I don't know. O is for the Old Man — the fear I'll be the same father my father was. P is for Pity — the fear of failure defining me in the eyes of others. R is for Reality — the fear that reality isn't what I dreamed it would be.

S is for Solitude — the fear of a loneliness that wasn't chosen. T is for Time — the fear of time slipping away. U is for Undoing — the fear of losing what I have. V is for Void — the fear of a freedom with no guarantees. W is for Weakening — the fear of trust being broken.

And this alphabet — this incomplete, imperfect alphabet of fear — is a map of my life. Because every one of those words — every fear — is part of me. A part that can't be removed, but can be understood. And understanding — as I've written many times — is the first step toward freedom.

THE FOX'S OTHER SIDE

Each of us has two faces — the one we show the world and the one we hide. The first is learned, polished, safe. The second is chaotic, unpredictable, full of contradictions. And that second — the hidden one — is more real than the first.

I once had an experience that made it clear. I was at a party — big, loud, full of people. I stood in a corner, holding a beer, watching the crowd. And then I noticed a guy — maybe thirty, in a suit, with a smile on his face. He talked to everyone, laughed with everyone, slapped everyone on the back. He seemed perfect — the kind of man everyone likes, everyone wants to drink with, everyone wants to approach.

And then — I don't know why — I looked into his eyes. And in his eyes I saw something the smile on his face didn't betray: exhaustion. Not physical — emotional. The kind of exhaustion that appears when you've been pretending to be someone you're not for too long. When you've worn a mask too heavy for too long. When you've said "yes" for too long when you wanted to say "no."

And I thought: "That guy is more broken than me." Not because he's a worse person — quite the opposite. But because I at least know I'm pretending. He may not even know. He may live believing that mask — the smiling, social, perfect mask — is who he is. And that's perhaps the saddest thing that can happen to a person: to live your whole life believing pretense is truth.

Because true courage isn't about telling the world the truth. It's about telling the truth to yourself. And that truth — that quiet, painful, not-always-beautiful truth — is the only thing that sets us free.

THE FEAR OF LOSS

There's something that connects every chapter of this book — and it's fear. Fear of loss. Fear of pain. Fear that when we open our eyes, everything we believed in will turn out to be an illusion.

This fear accompanies us from childhood. From the first moment we realized our mother could leave — even for a moment — we felt the shudder that follows us our whole life. The same shudder we feel when we lose a job, when a partner leaves, when a friend goes silent instead of answering a message. Because loss — every loss — is like returning to that first moment: the moment the world stops being safe.

I remember the first time I feared loss. I was maybe six. My parents went shopping — for an hour or two — and left me with my grandmother. Grandma was kind, patient, made soup. But I couldn't stop thinking my parents wouldn't come back. That they'd left me. That maybe they'd decided they didn't want to see me anymore. And that fear — irrational, illogical, absurd — was so strong I couldn't eat, couldn't speak, could do nothing but wait.

When they returned — arms full of bags, smiles on their faces, asking "How'd you like grandma's?" — I felt such relief that I started crying. And my grandmother, watching me, said: "See? They always come back." And that one phrase — "they always come back" — became my anchor for years. Until the day it turned out they don't always come back.

Because the truth is that loss is an inseparable part of life. You can't avoid it, can't hide from it, can't not feel it. You can only walk through it — with dignity, with courage, with hope that after the storm comes sun. But for that hope to mean anything, you first have to walk through the storm. Not around it, not over it, not pretending it isn't there. Through it — step by step, day by day, rain in your face and wind at your back.

The fear of loss is rooted so deep in us that we often don't realize how it steers us. We choose safe relationships instead of real ones — because real relationships demand openness, openness demands trust, and trust demands making peace with the idea that someone might hurt us. We choose a stable job over dreams — because dreams are uncertain, and uncertainty is like that childhood shudder we never want to feel again.

But the longer I live, the more I understand that avoiding loss is really avoiding life. Because life — real life, not the pretend one, not the safe one, not the controlled one — is full of losses. And each one, however painful, carries a lesson that can't be learned any other way.

CHAPTER II — LOVE WITHOUT ILLUSIONS

THE FAIRYTALES THEY TEACH US TO BELIEVE

True love almost never resembles what a person learns in childhood. When you sit before a TV screen at night or listen to stories told by elders — in those stories everything always resolves into a logical and just whole, where good is rewarded and the feeling between two people can survive any obstacle. But reality knows no such simple patterns.

The first film I watched that made me believe in love — the big, cinematic kind, with an ending where two people run toward each other against a setting sun — I was maybe ten. I remember sitting in my room after it and thinking: "It'll be just like that. Someday I'll meet someone and it'll be like that." And that belief stayed with me for years — patiently, silently, waiting for reality to confirm it.

Reality never confirmed it. Not because love doesn't exist — but because that cinematic version of love doesn't exist. It's fiction, just like superheroes and happy endings where no one gets sick, no one ages, and no one says "sorry, I made a mistake" without the camera pulling away and music swelling.

I first felt that gap between my imagining of love and its real shape. I believed in a simple rule: if a person is sincere, devoted, and ready to sacrifice, then sooner or later they'll meet someone who answers in exactly the same way. But the longer I watched the people around me, the more often I saw that their relationships were far more complicated — full of misunderstandings, hidden intentions, and small tensions capable of destroying even the most promising feeling.

The greatest illusion that accompanies a person at the start of every relationship is the belief that the other person sees the world exactly the way you do. That they understand your intentions without explanation. That they'll read your silence as accurately as they read your laugh. Only over time do we begin to understand that every person carries their own story, their own fears, and experiences that shape how they perceive feelings and relationships.

That's why the first step toward understanding love is abandoning the fairytales that shaped our expectations for years. Because only then can you see the other person as they truly are — not as a hero of an ideal story, but as a being full of contradictions, weaknesses, and moments of strength that appear when someone dares to look at them without illusions.

FIRST TRUE LOVE

First true love doesn't look the way you imagine. There are no fireworks, no soundtrack. No romantic walks along a rainy sea. Instead there's something no film shows — the moment you look at the other person and know you can't hide anything from them. And that they know more about you than anyone else — and stay anyway.

My first true love was named Kasia. Not the Kasia from the chapter on loyalty — a different Kasia. I met her at a party at mutual friends'. I remember her standing in a corner of the kitchen, holding a glass of wine, watching people as if she were watching a film whose plot she knew but whose ending she was waiting for. I felt something strange — as if we were the only two people in the room who knew the party was fake.

The first months were beautiful — in the way things are beautiful before they've been tested. We talked for hours. About everything: childhood, fears, dreams, nonsense. Her laugh was like music I wanted to play on loop. Her hands in mine — like an anchor holding me to the ground while the world spun too fast.

But then came the moment everything changed. Not dramatically — no fight, no tears, no door slamming against the wall. One evening we just sat in silence — and that silence was no longer warm. It was empty. As if someone had taken everything out of it and left only a shell.

For the first time then I understood that love isn't a permanent state. It's something you have to tend — daily, in every gesture, in every word. And that when you stop, you don't notice right away. You notice only when it's too late — when you look at the other person and see a stranger.

With Kasia it ended differently. With Kasia it didn't end badly — it ended quietly. We sat at the table drinking tea and she said: "You know, I think we're not us anymore." And I knew she was right. Because we both knew something had ended — not from betrayal or lies, but from a silence that over time grew louder than words.

THE QUESTION THAT ALWAYS COMES

There's a moment in almost every conversation about relationships between people that repeats with astonishing regularity. It usually arrives when the talk drifts from general statements to more personal ground — when people start speaking about specific people and emotions. Then, almost always, the question appears: "Did you see her naked?"

I remember the first time I heard that question in the context of a relationship that for me was something much deeper than physical fascination. We sat at a table amid the roar of conversations. When the question was asked, I felt as if the whole space around me suddenly slowed — as if all other sounds faded, leaving me alone with the simplicity of that sentence, which in a few words reduced an entire relationship to its most superficial aspect.

I began to wonder why people so often perceive closeness exactly that way. Why the first measure of a relationship becomes the moment clothes come off rather than the moment the mask comes off. Perhaps because the body is visible and easy to understand, while true emotional closeness demands far more courage — because it means showing the other person not only who we are in moments of strength, but also who we are in moments of weakness.

It was in moments like that that I realized many people have never experienced closeness that doesn't begin with the body, but with conversations held late into the night. With glances that say more than words. With a silence that isn't awkward but calm and natural — as if two people could breathe in it in the same rhythm, without needing to prove anything to each other.

That's why when someone asks me today about nakedness in a relationship, I answer in my mind that true nakedness doesn't begin when someone takes off their clothes. It begins when they stop pretending to be someone they're not. When they let the other person see their fears, their mistakes, their imperfections — and aren't afraid of being rejected for it.

THE ART OF LISTENING

Listening is an art nobody teaches us. We learn to speak, read, write, count — but nobody tells us how to listen. Yet listening may be the most important skill a person can possess. Because how we listen defines how we understand — ourselves, others, the world.

I remember the moment I realized I couldn't listen. I was meeting a friend — Marta — who was going through a hard period. She was telling me about her problems — work, a relationship, family — and I, instead of listening, was planning in my head what I'd say. I already had answers ready, advice ready, comfort ready. And when she finished, I opened my mouth to say something wise — and noticed Marta looking at me with an expression that said: "You weren't listening at all."

And she was right. I wasn't listening. I listened to the sound of her voice, but I didn't hear her words. I heard words, but I didn't feel the emotions behind them. I was like a radio that receives a signal but doesn't process it — just transmits it onward, without understanding.

From that day I began to learn to listen. It isn't easy — because truly listening means giving someone space. It means holding back from judging, advising, comparing to your own experiences. It means simply being — with open ears, open heart, and closed mouth.

And I discovered that when I really listen — when I'm not planning my answer, not judging, not comparing — I hear more than ever before. I hear what isn't said: the pauses, hesitations, shifts in tone, the quiet breath. And in those unsaid things hides the truth — the real, uncensored truth people can't express in words.

NAKEDNESS OF THE BODY

Nakedness of the body is something that throughout human culture has always stirred enormous emotion, curiosity, and a certain tension. On one hand, another person's body is something completely natural. On the other, through years of upbringing and social norms, we learn to treat it as something hidden — something to be revealed only in certain situations and only to chosen people. As a result, the moment someone sees another's body without any covering begins to be treated as a symbol of closeness — as if revealing skin alone were proof of a special bond.

But the longer I watched relationships between people, the more I began to understand that nakedness of the body is very often merely a superficial form of closeness. A body can be seen very quickly, sometimes within hours of meeting. But knowing a person in a deeper sense requires far more time, patience, and a willingness to have conversations that aren't always easy.

There were times I watched people convinced they'd achieved something special just because they'd crossed the line of physical closeness. And yet a few weeks later it turned out they barely knew each other. They couldn't talk about hard things. They couldn't be silent together. They couldn't look at each other in ordinary everyday moments that hold nothing of romantic rapture.

The body is beautiful in its simplicity, yet at the same time it's only one of many layers of human closeness. Over time I began to understand that the real test of a relationship isn't the moment two people see each other's bodies — but the moment they can look at each other without any role, without the need to pretend to be stronger or wiser than they actually are.

There was such a moment in my life — with a woman whose name I won't say. I remember lying in bed with her, it was late, the city behind the window was silent. And suddenly I felt something strange — as if all distance between us had vanished. Not physical — that was minimal from the start. But emotional. As if we'd stopped being two people in one bed and become part of something larger. Something that can't be described in words, because words are too thick, too tangible, too human for something so delicate.

And I understood: that is the nakedness I was looking for — not nakedness of the body, but nakedness of existence. The moment two people stop being afraid and simply are.

NAKEDNESS OF THE SOUL

Nakedness of the soul is a far rarer experience than nakedness of the body. It demands from a person the courage many people never find in themselves. And that courage isn't measured by the strength of your voice, but by your ability to show another person everything that was carefully hidden for years — from the world, from acquaintances, from family, and often even from yourself.

Every person carries stories they never tell aloud. Memories that surface only when silence settles in. Questions they can't answer even after years. These are the moments of life that form our most private space — a space inaccessible to most people, because revealing it means admitting our own imperfection.

True closeness begins the moment someone decides to cross that line. It begins in long conversations held in the evening, when words stop being a tool of self-presentation and become an attempt to describe what's really happening inside. In conversations where sentences go unfinished, pauses fill with hesitation, and glances search for confirmation that the other person won't turn away the moment they see us as we actually are.

There was Anna. We saw each other for several months — ostensibly a normal relationship, no fireworks, but with a clear sense that there was more than ordinary chemistry between us. One evening, sitting on the balcony looking at the city, she told me something she'd never told anyone before. Her voice trembled, and every word sounded as if she were pulling it from some deep, sealed place inside her. When she finished, silence fell — but not the awkward silence I knew from other situations. It was a silence in which I felt something had changed between us. That we'd crossed a line beyond which there's no return to pretending.

That conversation lasted until three in the morning. Anna told me about her childhood — about a mother who couldn't say "I love you" because she'd never heard it herself. About a father who drank and hit, and the next day brought flowers and apologized. About the fear of closeness that followed her through her entire adult life — a fear so deep she couldn't name it until she heard her own words spoken aloud, in the dark, on a balcony, before someone she trusted.

And then, for the first time in my life, I felt that someone had truly trusted me. Not trusted me with words — trusted me with their pain. Gave me something sealed inside them for years and said: "Here I am. This is who I really am. No filters, no mask, no fairytales."

If nakedness of the soul comes first in a relationship — if two people first learn each other's fears and deepest thoughts — then everything else takes on entirely different meaning. Physical closeness stops being a goal in itself and becomes a natural extension of a trust that had already appeared between them.

THE VICTORY OF HONESTY

The victory of honesty appears in relationships not as a sudden triumphant moment, but as a process that unfolds slowly. In quiet gestures, in conversations held at night, in a silence that isn't awkward but full of meaning. It's the moment a person stops hiding their fears, uncertainty, and mistakes, and instead lets the other see their entire interior — not only what's beautiful and certain, but also the chaos of thoughts, the fatigue, the moments of doubt, and the hidden dreams.

In those moments begins true closeness, which no longer rests on mutual attraction of bodies but on mutual understanding. On the ability to see another person as they really are, not as we'd want them to be. Trust appears — not a verbal declaration, but the natural product of every honest conversation, every glance that says: "I see you and I accept you, even when you're weak."

I remember a moment I'd call the victory of honesty in my life. I was with the woman I wrote about earlier. We were walking home in the evening down a dark street, and suddenly it started to rain. The rain was warm, summer, and the drops fell on our faces, clothes, hair. And then she said: "You know, I'm afraid that one day you'll wake up and decide I'm not what you think I am."

I didn't answer right away. We walked on in the rain, silent, and that silence was like a written conversation — full of words we didn't say but both heard. And then I said: "I already did that. And I'm still here."

That was my victory — not big, not spectacular, not worth describing in any book. But mine. Because I told the truth — the hardest truth a person can tell another: "I see you as you are. And I choose you."

That kind of closeness becomes what remains after everything — after fights, misunderstandings, fears, mistakes. It doesn't appear in grand declarations, but in daily gestures: in the way someone holds you when you tremble with fear. In the way they listen when words aren't enough. In the way they simply are — expecting nothing in return.

When a person experiences such a real relationship, they know that the love they have is resistant to everything. To time, fatigue, loneliness, moments of doubt — because it's built on authenticity, which can't be bought, can't be forced, and can't be faked. That's the true victory — the victory of honesty.

THE END OF THE FAIRYTALE

Every fairytale has an end — even one that seemed endless. And that end rarely looks the way we imagined. There are no dramatic scenes, no tearful farewells, no music swelling to a finale. Instead there's something more painful — silence. The silence that begins after the last fight, after the last attempt to save something that no longer exists.

I had such a relationship — the one I wrote about in the context of freedom in loss. It didn't end in the moment we said "it's over." It ended much earlier — the moment we stopped talking to each other. Not literally — we talked every day. But those conversations were like skeletons — they had shape, they had structure, but they held no life.

I remember the last evening we sat together in the kitchen. She read a book, I scrolled my phone. Between us was a table — so close, and so far. And suddenly she raised her head and said: "I think we should talk." And I answered: "Yes." And we both knew what we'd talk about. And we both knew what the answer would be. But we did it anyway — because you can't end something without naming it.

The end of a fairytale isn't a failure. It's the moment you begin to understand that not all stories have happy endings — and that doesn't make them worse. Some stories end quietly, without fireworks, but with dignity. And that dignity is worth more than any cinematic finale.

WHEN LOVE IS ENOUGH

There's a moment in every relationship when you ask yourself: "Is this enough?" Not "is it perfect?" — because perfection doesn't exist. Not "is it like in the movies?" — because movies lie. But "is what we have enough for the rest of my life?"

That question usually appears after the first year, after the second, after the third. When the fireworks die out, when novelty ends, when routine slips between you like an uninvited guest. And then you face a choice: stay or leave. Stay and build something real — or leave and chase the next firework.

I remember a conversation with a friend — Tomek, who'd been in a relationship for ten years. We sat in a café drinking coffee, and suddenly Tomek said: "You know what's hardest about being with one person for ten years? Not the fights. Not the problems. The hardest part is that you have to choose the same person every single day. Not once, not twice — every day. And each day is a new choice: I'm with you because I want to, not because I have to."

That sentence changed my understanding of love. Because love isn't something you have — it's something you do. Every day, in every gesture, in every word, in every silence. It's like a garden — if you don't tend it, it fills with weeds. But if you give it attention, time, and care — it blooms. Not spectacularly, not dramatically, but quietly, calmly, with dignity.

Once I understood that love is a choice, not a state, I stopped chasing the ideal. I stopped comparing my relationship to the ones I see in films, among friends, on social media. I started looking at what I have — at that specific person I'm with — and asking: "Do I choose you today?" And every time the answer was "yes," I felt something deeper than fireworks. I felt peace. The real, deep kind of peace — as if someone had lifted the weight of expectations I'd never be able to meet.

CHAPTER III — BETRAYAL AND TRUST

THE BROKEN WORD

Betrayal doesn't begin at the moment someone breaks a promise. It begins much earlier — at the moment that promise is made without conviction. At the moment words become a tool of manipulation instead of a message of truth. And most often we don't notice that moment, because we're too busy listening to what we want to hear instead of what we're actually hearing.

I remember my old friend — a man I'd known since high school. For years we shared everything: secrets, dreams, fears, alcohol, sleepless nights. I was convinced he was that one person I could trust without reservation. And maybe it was true — for a while. But trust, like every resource, has its breaking point. And that point isn't crossed in one great act of betrayal, but in a thousand small neglects, lies, and omissions that over time build an impassable wall.

His name was Wojtek. We met in high school — the math teacher sat us at the same desk, and that's how it stayed. Four years of high school and three of university, inseparable. We spent vacations together, shared money when we ran out for food, and argued about things that seem ridiculous now — about music, about films, about whether God exists.

Wojtek was the type who always had to be right. Not in an aggressive way — in an irritating, quiet way, nodding his head and saying: "Yeah, I get it, but you know…" And then you knew nothing you said mattered, because Wojtek had already made up his mind. But I tolerated it, because apart from that he was a good person — at least that's what I thought.

The first signal came in our third year of university. Wojtek started disappearing — not literally, but in a way I noticed. Our meetings became rarer. His messages — shorter. And when we did meet, I felt a strange barrier — as if there were an invisible pane of glass between us, through which I saw his face but couldn't reach him.

I found out by accident. A mutual acquaintance — Asia — told me at a party, over beer, with an expression that suggested she regretted saying it: "You know, Wojtek says strange things about you. That you're detached from reality. That you don't know what you want. That you're not good enough."

Those words hit me like cold water. Not because they hurt — but because they fit everything I'd been feeling for months. The strange pauses in conversations, the short messages, the odd feeling that someone I trusted was building something behind my back.

When I finally learned he'd been saying things behind my back that could destroy my reputation and my relationships with other people, I didn't feel anger. I felt a strange emptiness — as if someone had carved a piece out of me that can't be replaced. Because betrayal hurts not because of what was done. It hurts because of what was lost: the feeling that somewhere in the world there's someone you can tell everything to without fearing the consequences.

I watched him that evening when the truth came out. In his eyes I saw something I'd never seen in him before — fear. Not fear of my reaction, but fear of having lost something that would never return. He knew he'd crossed a line beyond which there's no return. And he knew I knew it. And he knew I knew that he knew.

We met one last time in the bar where we'd drunk beer after graduation. We sat across from each other and were silent. Then he said: "I'm sorry." And I answered: "I know." And that was all — one word and one sentence closing seven years. There was no anger. No tears. Only emptiness — the same I'd felt when I learned of the betrayal. But now that emptiness had a different taste. It was the emptiness of an ending — the emptiness that forms when something that was, stops being.

THE MECHANISM OF BETRAYAL

Betrayal rarely comes from outside. Most often it's born inside the relationship, in the places we both neglected. In the silences we stopped filling. In the glances we started avoiding. In the questions we stopped answering honestly.

Betrayal is like water slowly seeping into a building's foundations. For a long time no damage is visible — the wall looks solid, the plaster holds, the windows close. But one stronger tremor, one crack in the wall of the relationship, is enough for the whole structure to collapse. And then it turns out the foundations were rotting for years.

I remember a woman I saw for several months. At first everything seemed ideal — we talked for hours, shared our stories, built something that seemed real. Then, gradually, I started noticing small discrepancies between what she said and what she did. Her words were beautiful, but her gestures told another story. Her hands tightened slightly when she spoke of her past. Her eyes looked down for a fraction of a second when I asked about things that seemed irrelevant.

I don't blame her. Betrayal often comes not from malice, but from inability. From the inability to be sincere in a world that rewards pretense. From a fear of rejection stronger than the need for authenticity. From habits that over time become second nature — so natural that a person stops noticing they're lying.

But there's something else in the mechanism of betrayal rarely spoken of. Betrayal has two perpetrators — the one who betrays, and the one who allows themselves to be betrayed. I'm not talking about guilt — I'm talking about responsibility. Because each of us has warning signals inside — intuition, hunches, secret whispers saying: "Something's wrong here." And each of us has a choice: listen to those whispers, or ignore them. And ignoring them is so common, so widespread, so human — that it's painful.

The betrayed always says: "I didn't know." And the truth is, they knew. Maybe they didn't know exactly. Maybe they didn't know when. Maybe they didn't know with whom. But they knew something was wrong. They felt it in their stomach, in their back, in the way their heart started beating faster when their partner reached for the phone. And instead of listening to that voice, they told themselves: "I'm overreacting. It's paranoia. They'd never do that."

TRUST AS A CHOICE

Trust isn't something we receive automatically. It's something we choose — and we choose it every day, in every interaction, in every gesture, in every word. It's like a fire that must be kept alive, because a moment of inattention is enough for it to die — and relighting it is far harder than the first spark.

I've learned that trust isn't the belief that someone will never betray me. It's the decision that despite the risk, despite past experience, despite fear, I'll stay open to another person. Because closing yourself against betrayal also means closing yourself against true closeness — and that's a price not worth paying.

One day, after years of betrayals and disappointments, I met a person who told me something I'd never heard before. They said: "Trust isn't something you give me. It's something I choose every day — and I chose you." That sentence changed my understanding of relationships. Because I understood that trust isn't a permanent state, but a continuous act of courage. A choice we make every day, despite all the reasons not to.

There was a moment in my life when I trusted someone for the first time in years. After Wojtek, after the betrayals, after the disappointments — I'd locked myself up like a safe. Let no one in. Let no one see me as I truly was. Because everyone I let in found a way to hurt me sooner or later. And I'd had enough of wounds.

But one day — and it was a day I remember exactly, because it was raining and the air smelled of gasoline from passing cars — I told someone the truth. The truth I'd held inside for years. The truth about myself, my fears, my mistakes, my weakness. I said it without a plan, without strategy, without expectation — I just let it out, like air from a burst balloon.

And that person — the one person I told — listened in silence. Didn't judge. Didn't comfort. Didn't advise. They just were. And in that presence I felt something I hadn't felt in years: peace. The real, deep kind of peace — as if someone had lifted a weight I'd carried so long I'd forgotten I had it.

LEARNING TO SAY NO

"No" is one of the shortest words in any language — and at the same time one of the hardest to say. Because "no" means a boundary. The boundary you place between yourself and the world. The boundary that says: "Here ends what I can give. I can't go further."

For years I couldn't say "no." I said "yes" — to everyone, always, every time. "Yes" to another shift at work. "Yes" to helping friends when I myself had no strength. "Yes" to meetings, parties, trips, to everything anyone asked. Because I was afraid that if I said "no," someone wouldn't like me. That someone would reject me. That someone would think I was selfish.

But one day — and it was a day I remember because it was raining and it was Friday — I said "no." For the first time in my life I told someone: "I can't. Sorry, but I can't." And that person — a coworker asking me for help with a project — looked at me and said: "Okay. I get it." And that was all. No scene, no suffering, no consequences. Just one word — "okay" — and the silence that followed.

And I understood that "no" isn't an act of aggression. It's an act of respect — toward yourself and toward the other person. Because when I say "yes" while wanting to say "no," I'm lying. And lying — even with good intentions — is a form of manipulation. Because the other person thinks I'm ready to help them, and I know I'm not. And that disproportion — between what I say and what I feel — builds a wall that grows deeper over time.

Learning to say "no" is one of life's most important lessons. Because only when you can say "no" — to the world, to others, to yourself — do you begin to live on your own terms. You start choosing instead of being chosen. You start deciding instead of reacting. And then — only then — do you feel you're the master of your life, not its slave.

ONE WORD TOO MANY

There's a saying: "Better to remain silent and seem a fool than to speak and remove all doubt." I don't remember who said it, but I remember how deeply I understood it — in the most painful possible way.

I had a situation where I said one word too many. At a party, over beer, among people I barely knew. I said something — a joke that seemed funny to me, but to someone else was a blow. I didn't see it right away. I saw it only a moment later — that person's face, their eyes suddenly empty, their lips pressed tight.

And I understood my words — those stupid, thoughtless words — had done harm. Harm that can't be undone. Because words, once spoken, don't come back. They're like arrows — they fly, hit their target, and no force can stop them.

From that day I try to weigh my words. I don't always manage — I'm human, and people speak without thinking. But every time I feel I'm about to say something I might regret, I remember that person's face at the party. And I close my mouth. Because one word too many can destroy more than silence.

THE ONE-SECOND RULE

There's a rule I've learned over the years — and it may be the simplest and hardest rule I know. It goes like this: before every reaction — before every word, every gesture, every judgment — stop for one second.

One second. Nothing more. One second of silence in which you ask yourself: "Is what I'm about to say or do true? Is it necessary? Is it good?"

That one second changed my life. Not dramatically — not overnight. But gradually, week by week, month by month, I began to notice the difference. Because when I say something after reflection — after that one second — the words are different from the ones I speak on reflex. More considered. More honest. More mine.

And that one second — that tiny, unremarkable second — is an act of courage. Because it demands that I slow down. That I stop reacting automatically. That I give myself time to understand — myself and the other person. And understanding, even in the smallest measure, is better than no understanding at all.

I don't always manage. Sometimes I say things I shouldn't. Sometimes I react on reflex instead of consciously. I'm human — and humans react on reflex because that's how they're wired. But every time I manage to take that one second, I feel I've done something right. Something that matters. Something that brings me closer to who I want to be.

THE LESSON AFTER BETRAYAL

Betrayal teaches more than trust. Because betrayal forces you to look at yourself — not just at the one who betrayed. It forces you to understand why you allowed yourself to be betrayed. Why you ignored the signals. Why you chose the comfort of a lie over the discomfort of truth.

After every betrayal I've experienced, I stood before the mirror and asked myself: "What in this relationship was real, and what was my own illusion?" And every time I discovered that part of the responsibility was on my side. Not for the betrayal — that is always the betrayer's choice. But for not wanting to see what was obvious. For preferring to believe a version of reality that was convenient for me.

There was a moment — after one of the greatest betrayals of my life — when I sat on the floor of an empty room and wept. Not from pain — from relief. Because I could finally stop pretending everything was fine. Finally I could be weak. Finally I could be myself — the true self, the hidden one, the one always underneath, beneath the layers of mask, beneath the layers of pretense, beneath the layers of "I'm fine."

Betrayal is like a broken bone — it heals, but a scar remains to remind you of the pain. That doesn't mean you should avoid all wounds. Because scars are proof we survived something real — and that we can survive it. Betrayal, however painful, taught me more about people and about myself than any successful relationship. It taught me to tell true closeness from its imitation, true trust from its shadow. And it taught me that despite all betrayals, despite all disappointments, it's still worth trying — because the alternative is closing yourself off, and that's the only defeat truly impossible to survive.

FORGIVENESS

Forgiveness is something everyone talks about and few understand. Because forgiveness isn't forgetting. It isn't excusing. It isn't saying "nothing happened." Forgiveness is a decision — the decision that the pain done to me will no longer steer my life.

I couldn't forgive for years. Everyone who hurt me went on a list — a long list growing every year, full of names and dates I remembered with surgical precision. I kept that list in my head, nurtured it like treasure, because it gave me a feeling of control. "I remember what you did to me" — that was my slogan. My shield. My weapon.

But one day I understood that the list didn't protect me from pain. It protected me from everything else — from closeness, from trust, from love. Because everyone who appeared in my life was automatically compared to those who'd hurt me. And every time someone got too close, the list switched on like an alarm: "Careful. Remember what happened last time."

Forgiveness began with one person — my father. Not because he'd done me the greatest harm — quite the opposite, his harms were quiet, subtle, hard to name. I began to forgive him for his silence. For never saying "I love you." For a love that couldn't be seen — because it was hidden behind advice, repairs, well-done deeds I loved but didn't understand until I became a father myself.

And then I forgave further. More people, more wounds, more names from the list. It didn't happen overnight — it was a process that lasted months, maybe years. But with every forgiveness I felt lighter. As if I were lifting stones off myself one by one, until finally I could stand straight and breathe with a full chest.

BUILDING FROM SCRATCH

Rebuilding trust after betrayal is like building a house on the ruins of an old one. You can't use the same materials — those materials failed. You can't raise the walls in the same places — the foundations are broken. You have to start over — from zero, with new plans, new materials, a new vision.

After years of solitude — years of learning to trust myself before trusting anyone else — I met someone. Someone just as weary as I was. Someone who looked at the world through shadow-filled eyes — not because they were a pessimist, but because they'd seen too much. And the two weary people sat at a table and said to each other: "Let's try. But differently. No fairytales, no expectations, no schedules. Just try."

And we tried. Day by day, conversation by conversation, gesture by gesture. We built trust like masons — brick by brick, day by day, without hurry, without declarations, without fanfare. And that slowness, that patience, that decision of daily choice — was more beautiful than any grand declaration of love.

Because trust — that kind of trust — doesn't come in a single moment. It comes in a thousand moments — in every gesture, every word, every silence that is calm instead of awkward. And when you finally understand that the person beside you isn't your enemy — that they aren't planning to hurt you, that they aren't waiting for the right moment to leave — then you feel something that has no name. Something between peace and gratitude. Something between relief and hope. And you know it was worth it — worth walking through all of it, to now sit at a table with someone you truly trust.

CONVERSATIONS AT NIGHT

The best conversations happen at night. Because night is the only time the world goes quiet — and in that silence, words take on different meaning. More honest, more personal, more true. Because at night there's no need to pretend. No masks, no roles, no expectations. Just darkness and voices — quiet, intimate, full of trust.

I remember a conversation held at night — with a friend I hadn't spoken to in years. He called at one in the morning — no reason, no occasion, he just called. And said: "I need to talk to you. Not about anything important. I just need to hear your voice."

And we talked — for two hours, about everything and nothing. About childhood, music, weather, people we'd met, mistakes we'd made, dreams we'd abandoned. And that conversation — that quiet, nocturnal, trusting conversation — was like balm. Like medicine for something I couldn't name.

Because night conversations are different from daylight ones. By day we talk about matters — work, problems, plans. At night we talk about ourselves — our fears, our hopes, our truths. And those truths — the nocturnal, raw, uncensored truths — are the only truths that really count.

THE SILENCE AFTER THE STORM

After every betrayal, every disappointment, every pain, comes a moment of silence. Not the silence I wrote about at the start of this book — the teacher, the mirror, the guide. This is a different silence. The silence of exhaustion. A silence in which you have the strength neither to speak nor to stay silent. A silence in which everything you knew about yourself and the world lies in ruins — and you don't know what to rebuild.

I had such a silence after one of the greatest betrayals of my life. I sat in an empty room — literally empty, because I'd sold the furniture — and stared at the wall. I didn't think. I didn't weep. I didn't plan. I just sat. And that idleness — that absolute, terrifying idleness — was the only thing I had the strength for.

But that silence, however painful, was necessary. Because only in it — in absolute emptiness, in total absence of stimulus — could I hear myself. Hear what I truly want. Hear who I truly am — not who I was in a relationship, not who I was at work, not who I was in the eyes of others. Who I am when left alone with myself.

And then, in that silence, I heard something strange: singing. Not literally — no music, no voices. But something inside me began to sing — quietly, timidly, like a child trying to sing a song for the first time. And that singing — that quiet, timid melody — was the beginning of something new. Something I can't name, but feel in every gesture, every word, every day I live now.

The silence after the storm is necessary. Because without it there's no rebuilding. Without it there's no new start. Without it we're like people building a house on the ruins of an old one — without waiting for the dust to settle so we can see what remains and what must be built anew.

CHAPTER IV — SOLITUDE, FREEDOM AND THE PASSING OF THINGS

CHOSEN SOLITUDE

There's a huge difference between imposed solitude and chosen solitude. Imposed solitude hurts — because it comes from lack, from rejection, from a situation where a person would like to be with someone and can't. Chosen solitude, on the other hand, is an act of courage — the decision that for a while I'll be alone, not because I must, but because I want to understand something that's invisible in the company of other people.

I spent many months in solitude, not because I had no one to call, but because I needed to hear myself. Without the noise of conversation, without expectations, without the need to react to someone's emotions. I needed silence — the same silence that at the start of this book was my teacher, and which over time became something far more important: essential to understanding myself.

In solitude, days look different. They lack the structure other people impose. No conversations at breakfast, no "what are you doing tonight?", no need to plan, organize, adjust to someone else's schedules. Only you — and time. And time, on its own, is terrifying, because there's nothing to fill it with but your own thoughts.

In the first weeks of solitude I fled from myself — I listened to podcasts, watched films, read books. Anything not to be left alone with the silence. But silence is patient. It doesn't flee. It waits. And when I finally had nowhere left to run — when the podcasts, films, and books ran out — I sat in an empty room and let it come.

And then, in that silence, I heard myself. For the first time in years.

In solitude I discovered that most of my relationships rested on mutual need, not mutual choice. People were near me because I gave them something — a sense of safety, entertainment, confirmation of their worth. And I was near them because they gave me something back: the feeling of not being alone, of being seen, of existing in someone's world. But that wasn't a true connection — it was trade, a transaction, an exchange of goods that dissolves the moment one side loses what it offers.

That truth hit me like cold water. Because if that wasn't closeness — then what was it? For years I'd lived in the illusion of a convenient arrangement, calling it a relationship.

One evening, sitting in an empty room, the phone rang. A number I didn't know. I answered. On the other side was a woman — a stranger, with a voice that sounded like exhaustion. She said: "Sorry, wrong number." And hung up. And that conversation — that one second, those two words "wrong number" — was more real than all the conversations I'd had the past year with people pretending to know me.

FREEDOM IN LOSS

True freedom doesn't begin when we gain everything we dream of. It begins when we lose everything we considered essential — and discover we still exist. That we can still breathe, think, feel. That the world didn't end, even though everything I'd built for years lay in ruins.

When I lost my job, my partner, and my apartment within one year, I got the chance to test that theory in practice. And to my surprise, the theory turned out true. The moment everything else disappeared, I felt something I hadn't expected: relief. Not because I enjoyed suffering. Because I could finally stop being afraid. Afraid of loss. Because the loss had already happened — and it turned out to be bearable.

First came the job. The layoff — formal, polite, with a three-month notice period and a card reading "thank you for your cooperation." I sat in the office staring at my computer screen, feeling a strange emptiness — not from losing the job, but from realizing how many years I'd devoted to something that meant nothing. For five years I got up in the morning, drove to the office, did what I was told, and came home. And suddenly it turned out those five years could have been days — one day repeated fifteen hundred times.

A week after the layoff, my partner said: "I'm leaving." No fight, no drama. Pain in her eyes, but determination in her voice. She said: "I can't live anymore with someone who doesn't know who he is." And she was right. Because I didn't know. I didn't know who I was outside of work, outside the relationship, outside the roles I played. I was like an actor taking off his costume and standing on an empty stage — not knowing what to say, because all his lines were written by someone else.

I sold the apartment a month later. Not because I had to — because I couldn't stay there. Because that home was our home — mine and hers — and every wall remembered our conversations, our silences, our fights and reconciliations. And those walls, instead of comforting me, screamed at me: "You're alone."

But freedom — the real freedom — came unexpectedly. When I'd lost everything, I got up one morning, left the house, and didn't know where to go. And that uncertainty — that absolute, terrifying uncertainty — was like fresh air after years in a sealed room. I didn't know who I was. I didn't know what I wanted. I didn't know where I was going. But for the first time in my life I felt no fear about it. I felt curiosity.

That doesn't mean it was easy. It was hellishly hard. Every morning I woke with a feeling of emptiness that seemed impossible to fill. But over time that emptiness began to change. It began to become space — a place where I could put something new, something truly mine, not something I'd borrowed from the world in exchange for a false sense of safety.

True freedom is terrifying, because it gives no guarantees. It doesn't tell you who you are, doesn't give you a map, doesn't point a direction. It forces you to find the answers yourself — and accept that those answers may differ from the ones you expected.

THE PASSING OF THINGS AS A TEACHER

Death is the only certainty we possess — and at the same time the only one whose consequences we ignore for most of our lives. We live as if we had endless time. We put off until later the conversations we should have today. We apologize for things we should have said yesterday. And we love people we should love now — not tomorrow, not next week, not next year.

I first encountered death in a way that touched me personally when my grandfather died. It wasn't a sudden death — he'd been ill for months. But even so, his death struck me with a force I didn't expect. Because with him disappeared a whole world — a world with specific smells, specific stories, specific gestures that had been part of my life from the beginning. A world that existed for no one else the way it existed for me — and now existed only in my memory.

Grandfather was a small man — not in height, but in the way he occupied space. He didn't walk into a room demanding attention. He entered quietly, sat in his chair, and waited for someone to come to him. And when you came, he didn't say much. He talked about the weather, about the vegetables in the garden, about the neighbor who was at it again at midnight. But in those simple sentences hid something I discovered only after his death — a whole philosophy of life, packed into sentences about the weather and cucumbers.

I remember one day sitting with him in his garden — the same one where for thirty years he'd grown tomatoes, cucumbers, and raspberries. Grandfather was silent, I was silent. The sun blazed, bees buzzed over the flowers, and we sat in the shade of an old apple tree watching a world that didn't need our words. After a long while grandfather said: "You know, the most important thing in life isn't what you do. The most important thing is how you'll remember what you did."

I didn't understand that sentence then. I thought grandfather was talking about history — about leaving a mark behind. But now, years later, I know he was talking about something else. About memory. About the fact that everything we have — really have — is memories. And that those memories are the only thing that remains when everything else disappears.

Death isn't an abstraction. It's something extremely concrete — the loss of concrete things: a voice, a smell, a touch, the way someone said my name. And that concreteness makes the passing of things not a theory, but an experience that shapes how we live — if we let it.

WHAT REMAINS

When I think about the passing of things, I don't think about great things. I don't think about achievements, money, status. I think about small moments — a friend's laugh, the touch of a hand, the warm light of a lamp on a winter evening. About all those moments that seem insignificant while they last, and only in retrospect turn out to be the only things that really count.

I remember my grandfather's coffee — the cheap kind, from a sack, brewed in an old kettle. I remember my mother singing while washing dishes — always the same song, always slightly off-key. I remember the warmth of my father's hands — the same hands that fixed taps and built furniture — when he placed one on my head and said: "Everything will be all right." I don't remember whether I believed it. But I remember the warmth of that hand.

These are the things that remain. Not achievements. Not titles. Not money. The smell of coffee. Singing over the dishes. The warmth of a hand on one's head. And the silence — the silence that filled the room when everyone was present but no one spoke. Because that silence was a message in itself — a message saying: "We're together. And that's enough."

The passing of things teaches that life isn't a quantity, but the quality of lived moments. It's not about how many years we live, but how we live those years. About whether we allow ourselves authenticity or hide behind masks. About whether we tell the people we love that we love them — or wait for the "right moment" that never comes.

OLD AGE IS NOT A SENTENCE

Old age isn't something that comes overnight. It's something that accumulates over the years — in wrinkles, gray hair, aching backs, in a fatigue that doesn't go away after one night of rest. But old age isn't only physical. It's also emotional — and that emotional old age is more terrifying than the physical one.

Because emotional old age means resignation. It means saying: "I'll learn nothing new. I'll experience nothing new. Nothing will change." And that resignation — that quiet, slow, elusive resignation — may be the worst thing that can happen to a person. Because it means they've stopped growing. Stopped learning. Stopped being curious.

But old age doesn't have to look that way. I've known people — old, gray-haired, with aching knees, with wrinkles on their faces — who were younger in spirit than many thirty-year-olds. Because they didn't give in to resignation. They didn't say "nothing new." Every day they learned something new — a new book, a new recipe, a new word, a new way of looking at the world.

And those people — those rebellious, curious, full-of-life elders — were proof to me that old age isn't a matter of years. It's a matter of attitude. And that attitude — that curiosity, that openness, that willingness to learn — is something you can nurture your whole life. No matter how old you are.

THE HARMONY OF OPPOSITES

Life is a paradox. It's simultaneously beautiful and painful, joyful and sad, simple and complex. And that contradiction — that harmony of opposites — may be the most beautiful thing in existence.

Because how else to explain that the most beautiful moments of life are often born from pain? That the strongest bonds form after loss? That the deepest understanding comes after the deepest darkness?

I remember the moment that made it clear. I stood in a cemetery — at my grandfather's grave. Rain was falling, people stood in silence, and I looked at the headstone thinking about everything I'd lost. And suddenly I felt something strange: gratitude. Not for death — for life. For the fact that this man existed. That he was part of my life. That he gave me something I carry inside to this day.

And that gratitude — that quiet, subtle gratitude in the middle of pain — was like light in a dark room. It didn't illuminate everything — didn't give answers, didn't show the way. But it gave something: meaning. Meaning born from the harmony of opposites — from the fact that pain and beauty, loss and gain, death and life are connected so tightly that one can't exist without the other.

THE MOMENTS WE REMEMBER

There are moments we remember not because they were important, but because they were real. Moments when everything else disappeared — obligations, problems, fears — and only we remained, present, here and now, without a mask, without a role, without the need to pretend.

I remember such a moment — ordinary, everyday, not worth describing in any book. I sat on a bench in a park. It was summer, the sun blazed, and I was eating ice cream — the cheap kind, from a kiosk, chocolate. And then a girl walked past me — a stranger, unknown, pushing a wheelbarrow full of flowers. She looked at me and smiled. It wasn't a romantic smile, not a smile of desire. Just a smile — warm, human, full of kindness. And that smile — that one second, that one glance, that one spark of human connection — stayed with me for years.

Because people remember not the great events — but the small moments. Not the fights — but the reconciliations. Not the achievements — but the failures that taught them something important. Not the words — but the silences in which they felt safe.

And that truth — that simple but powerful truth — changes how we live. Because if we know we'll remember the small moments, we begin to appreciate them. We begin to pay attention to what really matters: a stranger's smile, the warmth of a hand, the smell of coffee, the silence that fills a room when someone is beside you. And then life — this ordinary, everyday, imperfect life — becomes something beautiful. Not because it's perfect — but because it's ours.

THE FEAR OF DEATH

The fear of death isn't really a fear of death. It's fear of what remains after us. Fear of being forgotten. Fear that our life didn't matter. Fear of the void that will come when we close our eyes and never open them again.

I had such a night — one, a single night in my life, when I truly feared death. Not because I was sick. Not because I was in danger. I just lay in bed in the dark, and suddenly it hit me — with full force, with full brutality — that one day I'll die. That this room I'm lying in will one day be empty. That the people I love will one day speak of me in the past tense. That the world will go on existing — without me. And that "without me" was so terrifying, so incomprehensible, that I couldn't breathe.

I sat on the bed, face in my hands, and wept. Not quietly, not neatly — I wept like a child, loudly and uncontrollably. Because that truth — that single truth that can't be ignored, rationalized, or hidden — hit me with such force that everything else lost meaning.

But that night — that one night — was the most important night of my life. Because when I got up in the morning, for the first time in a long while, I felt something strange: gratitude. Gratitude for being alive. For being able to breathe. For the sun shining. For someone once saying "good morning" to me — and for that "good morning" being real. Because when you realize everything is temporary — that even pain, even fear, even loneliness is temporary — then you begin to appreciate every moment. Even the most ordinary ones. Even the ones that seem insignificant.

THE LAST DAY OF SUMMER

There's a day each year that always moves me — not because something happened on it, but because something ends. It's the first day of autumn — the moment summer leaves and autumn slips in quietly, without permission, without farewell.

The first day of autumn always smells different from other days. The air is cooler, the sun lower, the shadows longer. And in that smell — the smell of damp earth, fallen leaves, cold wind — hides something with no name. Something between longing and hope. Between regret and gratitude. Between an ending and a beginning.

I remember the first day of autumn when I was a child. I was walking home from school — it was warm, but no longer hot. The leaves were beginning to change — from green to yellow, from yellow to red. And I, walking the same road I walked every day, felt something strange — as if something had ended. I didn't know what. I couldn't name it. But I felt that summer — with its carefreeness, its freedom, its warmth — was leaving. And that autumn — with its chill, its silence, its reflection — was coming.

And that moment — that transition between one and the other — was like a metaphor for life. Because life is a series of transitions — between childhood and adulthood, between youth and old age, between hope and disappointment, between love and loss. And those transitions — however painful — are necessary. Because without them there's no growth. Without them there's no change. Without them there's no life.

The last day of summer is like a farewell — quiet, subtle, almost invisible. But it's a farewell that carries a promise: the promise that something new is coming. That autumn will bring its gifts — its colors, its smells, its silence. And that that silence — the autumnal, reflective silence — will be just as beautiful as the summer rustle of leaves.

MEANING IN MEANINGLESSNESS

The greatest question a person can ask isn't "why do I live?" — but "how to live, since everything ends anyway?" Because the answer to the first question is simple — either "I don't know" or "for someone." But the answer to the second is hard, because it requires accepting that life has no "meaning" in the sense we seek meaning. No purpose. No finale. No reward at the end.

For years I searched for meaning — in work, in love, in achievements, in money, in alcohol, in books, in conversations with strangers in bars at two in the morning. And every time I thought I'd found it, it turned out it wasn't that meaning. That it was only a substitute — a shadow of true meaning cast on a cave wall, while the real source of light was somewhere else.

I searched for meaning in great things — grand decisions, grand changes, grand moments. And meaning was in the small things. In morning coffee. In a ray of sun on the wall. In a message from someone who remembers. In the glance of a stranger on the street that for a fraction of a second is full of kindness. In a silence that isn't empty but full — full of everything we didn't say but both know.

I understood this only after loss — after losing everything I considered essential. And then, at the bottom, in an emptiness that seemed unbearable, I found something I didn't expect: myself. The real me — not the one with the mask, not the one with the role, not the one pretending. Just me — an ordinary, tired, error-prone man sitting on the floor of an empty room looking out a window.

And that man — that ordinary, imperfect man — had something inside he didn't have before: peace. Not the peace that comes from control, but the peace that comes from acceptance. From accepting that life has no meaning — and that this is fine. Because meaning isn't something you find. Meaning is something you create — daily, in every gesture, in every word, in every silence.

WHEN WORDS AREN'T ENOUGH

There are moments when words aren't enough. When the other person is crying and you don't know what to say. When someone close is leaving and no words can comfort. When the world collapses around you and you stand in the ruins knowing no sentence will change what happened.

In such moments the only thing you can do is be present. Say nothing. Don't advise. Don't comfort. Just be — beside, close, quiet. Because presence — that quiet, silent, invaluable presence — is worth more than any word.

I remember the moment I understood that. I sat with a friend — Marta — who had just lost her mother. Marta cried, and I didn't know what to say. I tried — "It'll be okay," "Time heals wounds," "Your mother was a wonderful woman" — but every word sounded false. Like empty words containing nothing but sound.

And then I stopped talking. I sat beside her, took her hand, and was silent. For a long, long moment — maybe twenty minutes, maybe an hour — we sat in silence. And that silence — that shared, warm, understanding silence — did more than any word. Because that silence said: "I'm here. I'm not leaving. I'm not judging. I'm just here."

And from that day I know that sometimes the best thing I can do is close my mouth and listen. Not listen to words — because words often aren't enough. But listen to the silence — because in silence hides the truth that words can't express.

MORNING AFTER THE DARK NIGHT

The worst nights are the ones you can't sleep through. The ones where thoughts circle like mosquitoes — constant, persistent, unswattable. The ones where you stare at the ceiling and see your life like a film — but not the nice, warm film with a happy ending. The raw, merciless documentary where all your mistakes, all your failures, all your missed chances are shown in slow motion, without music, without commentary, without a merciful frame.

I had such a night — one of the worst. I lay in bed thinking about everything that had gone wrong. The job I'd lost. The relationship that ended. The friends who drifted away. The parents I can't tell what I feel. Myself — about who I am and who I wanted to be, and who I never became.

And at some point, maybe four in the morning, when darkness was thickest and silence loudest, I thought: "This will end someday. This pain. This night. These thoughts. One day I'll wake up and it'll be different." I didn't know how it would be different. I didn't know if it would be better. But I knew it would be different — because everything changes. Even what seems unchangeable. Even what seems eternal.

And that thought — that simple but powerful thought — was like light in a dark room. It didn't illuminate everything — didn't give answers, didn't show the way. But it gave one thing: hope. Hope that this night will end. That morning will come. That the sun will rise — as it always rises, no matter what we do, what we feel, what we lose.

The morning after a dark night is different from every other morning. Not because it's more beautiful — it isn't always. But because it's lived consciously. Because after a night when you thought you wouldn't wake up, every light, every sound, every breath becomes a gift. And then you understand what it means to live — not survive, not function, not fill the days. To live. With the full awareness that every moment is unique and will never repeat.

THE MORNING AFTER THE NIGHT

The most beautiful mornings are the ones that follow the worst nights. Because those mornings — those quiet, calm, light-filled mornings — are like a promise. The promise that everything will change. That the night is over. That the day is coming.

I remember the morning after one of the worst nights of my life. I lay in bed, unable to sleep, unable to think, unable to do anything. And then — suddenly, without warning — the sun rose. And its light — warm, golden, gentle — poured through the window and fell on my face.

And then, under that light, I felt something strange: peace. Not the peace of solved problems — the problems were still there. Not the peace of hope — hope was fragile. But the peace of presence. Of the fact that I am. That I'm alive. That the sun will rise — as it always rises, no matter what I feel.

And that morning — that ordinary, everyday morning — was like a gift. Like a reward for surviving the night. Like a promise that tomorrow will be different — not better, not worse, but different. And that difference — that change, that constant change — is the only constant in life.

THE MORNING ROUTINE

Every morning I do the same thing. I get up, go to the kitchen, switch on the kettle, put a cup on the counter, and wait for the water to boil. And in that simple, repeating act — in that waiting for the boil — hides the whole meaning of my life.

Because that morning routine — that quiet, unassuming, daily routine — is like an anchor. It holds me to the ground while the world spins too fast. It reminds me that I'm here — here and now — and that this moment, this one moment of waiting for tea, is enough.

I didn't always think that way. Once the morning routine was a burden — something to get through before real life began. But over time I understood that the routine is life. Not preparation for life — life itself. Because life isn't great events — it's small habits, small gestures, small moments we repeat every day.

And that morning tea — that simple, cheap, daily tea — is my daily triumph. Not a great triumph — a small, quiet, unassuming one. But mine. And it's enough.

CHAPTER V — FAMILY AND ROOTS

THE HOUSE YOU LEAVE

Each of us carries inside the house we left — not just the physical one, with specific walls and an address, but the emotional one, built of habits, patterns, silences, and sayings we heard so often they became part of how we think about the world. That house is in us even if we moved thousands of kilometers from where we were born. It's in us in how we react to stress, in our fears, in our expectations of people and of ourselves.

I grew up in a house where little was said about feelings, but much about duty. Love was expressed through deeds — a meal set on the table, a fixed tap, the silence in which my father sat beside me when I needed help but didn't know how to ask. I don't remember my father ever saying "I love you" — but I remember his hands, always first to work and last to rest. That was his way of saying what he couldn't say in words.

My father worked in a factory — three shifts, five days a week, for thirty years. He didn't complain. Didn't talk about work. Didn't say he was tired. He just got up at five in the morning, ate breakfast — always the same: bread with butter and coffee — left, and returned at six, sometimes eight, if there was overtime. And when he came home — tired, dirty, eyes watering from dust — he sat in his chair and asked: "How was school?" And I told him. About math, about PE, about friends. And he listened. Didn't comment, didn't advise, didn't judge. He just listened — and that was all I needed.

My mother was his mirror image — a woman who devoted her whole life to family, and then suddenly, when the children left, stood before an empty apartment and didn't know who she was when she wasn't "mom." Her world was shrinking before her eyes: the jars of pickled cucumbers went empty, a drawer of recipes replaced the calendar full of school dates, and the silence in the apartment was so loud it interfered with the TV. She began searching for something she couldn't name — signed up for a floristry course, started going on bus tours, kept a diary in which she wrote down everything she remembered from her youth. She was looking for herself — the version that existed before she became a wife and mother.

I remember the day I called my mother and heard something strange in her voice — lightness. She said: "You know, today I went for a walk by myself. No plan, no destination. I just walked and looked at the trees. And you know what? I liked it." And I understood then that my mother — that woman who'd devoted her whole life to others — had finally found something for herself. And that "something" was as simple as a walk without a destination.

THE PATTERNS WE CARRY

Family gives us not only love and support — it gives us patterns we often repeat unconsciously. The way our parents handled stress, conflict, emotions — becomes our way, unless we consciously decide to change it.

I noticed it in myself — in how I react to arguments. I always go quiet. I always retreat. I always give the other person time and space instead of saying what I feel. And I always thought it was my trait — something from my nature. One evening, talking with a therapist, I heard something that changed everything: "That's not your trait. That's a pattern you learned." A pattern I learned from my father, who in turn learned it from his father.

And I felt a strange relief — because if it's a pattern, it means I can change it. I don't have to be condemned to repeating the same mistakes, to being closed-off like the men before me. I can choose differently — I can choose openness, a choice no one in my family made before me.

But changing a pattern isn't a process that happens overnight. It's like learning to walk — you fall, you get up, you fall, you get up. And every time you fall it's a little lighter, because you know you know how to get up. I remember the first argument in which I didn't fall silent. The first time in my life I said: "I'm scared. I don't know what to say, but I'm scared." And that other person — the one I was arguing with — looked at me as if seeing me for the first time. And said: "I'm scared too." And then the argument ended — not because we'd found a solution, but because we'd stopped being opponents and become two frightened people looking for a way toward each other.

THE SECRET OF A FATHER'S SILENCE

My father wasn't silent by choice. He was silent from necessity — because no one taught him how to talk about feelings. His father — my grandfather — was even more silent. A man from a generation in which a guy didn't cry, didn't complain, didn't say "it hurts me." A guy who pulled himself together and kept going — with an aching back, an empty wallet, a heart full of pain no one wanted to hear.

I remember one evening — I was maybe fourteen — when I came home from school crushed. I don't remember what happened — maybe a fight with a friend, maybe a bad grade, maybe a girl who didn't return my glance. I don't remember. But I remember walking into the kitchen, and my father sat at the table drinking beer and staring out the window.

I sat down across from him. I was silent. He was silent. For a long moment no one said anything. Then my father raised his eyes, looked at me, and said: "Bad?"

That one word — "Bad?" — was the only question he knew how to ask. Not "What happened?", not "Tell me about it", not "How can I help?" Just "Bad?" And I nodded. And he nodded. And that was all — our entire conversation. One word and two nods.

But in that moment — in that silence, in that one word, in those two nods — I felt something I hadn't felt in a long time: understanding. My father couldn't say "I understand you, son." But he could say "Bad?" — and that in itself was a form of understanding. Because who asks "Bad?" knows that someone beside them is suffering. And knows that the only thing they can do is be beside them.

And that silence — that quiet, silent presence of my father — was perhaps the most beautiful gift I ever received from him. Not because it was perfect — it wasn't. But because it was real. Because my father gave me what he had: his presence. And that presence — despite the silence, despite the lack of words — was enough.

THE WEIGHT OF SILENCE

Silence has its weight. It isn't always light — sometimes it's heavy as stone. Because silence can be an act of courage — but it can also be an act of cowardice. It can be a form of respect — but also a form of punishment.

I remember my father's silence — the same one I wrote about earlier. For years I thought that silence was an act of love — that my father was quiet because he couldn't speak, but loved me in his own way. But one day I understood that silence has another side too. That my father was silent not because he couldn't speak — but because he was afraid to speak. Because speaking meant risking rejection. And rejection — for a man of his generation — was the worst thing that could happen.

And that truth — that quiet, painful truth — changed the way I understand silence. Because silence isn't always an act of love. Sometimes it's an act of fear. And the difference — between silence out of love and silence out of fear — is enormous. Because silence out of love is warm, present, full of care. And silence out of fear is cold, distant, full of dread.

And my own silence — my own — is a mixture of both. Sometimes I'm silent because I love. And sometimes because I'm afraid. And that ambiguity — that uncertainty about whether I'm silent out of love or out of fear — may be the hardest thing to understand in myself.

CONTINUITY AND BREAKING

Family is like a chain — it stretches through generations, passing on not only genes, but also beliefs, fears, desires, and habits. Some links of that chain are strong and beautiful — traditions, values, a sense of belonging. Others are weak and painful — violence, alcoholism, the emotional unavailability passed from generation to generation like a contagious disease.

Each of our tasks is to understand which links we want to keep and which we must break. Because if we don't make a conscious decision, if we don't stop and ask ourselves: "What of what I inherited is truly mine, and what is just a habit I repeat because that's how I was taught?" — then we'll live a life that isn't ours. We'll repeat someone else's patterns, fulfill someone else's dreams, carry someone else's fears — without even knowing we're doing it.

My grandfather was silent in arguments. My father was silent in arguments. And I was silent in arguments. Three generations of men who couldn't say "what you're saying hurts me." Three generations of people who, instead of words, used silence — the quiet, dense, impassable silence that was like a wall between us and the people we loved.

But the chain isn't condemned to continuity. It can be broken — but it must be broken consciously, deliberately, aware that breaking it hurts. Because breaking a link isn't an easy gesture — it's a decision that demands the courage to say: "Enough. I won't live like this. I choose differently." And that decision is painful, because it means leaving what's familiar, what's safe, what's "normal" in your family. But it's also the only road to freedom.

One day, looking at my child, it hit me that I am now the one who creates the home — the emotional one, built of my habits, my reactions, my silences and my words. That for everything I pass on — every moment of presence, every unspoken word, every warm night when my child curls up against me, and every argument in which I say something I shouldn't — I will be responsible. Not before God, not before society, but before that small person who will one day carry their home inside them.

THE CHOICE OF FATHERHOOD

Fatherhood isn't something you receive. It's something you choose — every day, in every gesture, in every word, in every silence. And that choice may be the hardest choice a person can make — because it concerns not only himself, but a small person who in a few years will carry an image of a father inside — the same image I carry from my own father.

When my child was born, I stood by the hospital bed and looked at that small creature — red face, clenched fists, crying so loudly it seemed to be telling the world: "I'm here! I'm here!" And I felt something I didn't expect: fear. Not fear of responsibility — fear of myself. Because suddenly I realized I'd have to give this child something I never received: words. The words "I love you." The words "I'm proud of you." The words "I'm sorry."

For the first months of fatherhood I did everything to be a better father than my father. I read books on parenting. Listened to parenting podcasts. Went to workshops. And then one evening, when my child wouldn't fall asleep, I sat with them in my arms and began to speak — not to them, because they were three months old and didn't understand words — but to myself. I said: "I don't know how to do this. I don't know how to be a father. Because nobody taught me. But I promise you one thing — I'll try. Every day I'll try."

And that promise — that quiet, unspoken promise — became the foundation of my fatherhood. Because I have no ready answers. No manual. No pattern to hold onto. I only have myself — with all my mistakes, my fears, my imperfections — and the awareness that despite all that, I'll try. Because the alternative is repeating what was. And I don't want that.

WHAT CHILDREN TEACH US

Children are teachers — maybe the best I've ever met. They teach us things we forgot: curiosity, simplicity, presence. They teach us to look at the world with eyes that don't yet know disappointment.

My son — maybe three at the time — was watching the rain through the window one day. It was pouring, the street was empty, the sky gray. And he stood at the window, nose to the glass, saying: "Dad, look! The water is dancing!"

And I looked through the window — and saw the same thing he did: raindrops falling on puddles, forming circles that spread and vanish. But I saw it as "bad weather." He saw it as "a dance." And that difference — between my pessimism and his optimism — was like a mirror in which I saw how much I'd changed.

Because when I was small, I also saw a dance in the rain. I also looked at the world with wonder, with curiosity, with joy. But growing up — with its rules, its expectations, its disappointments — took that ability from me. Took the eyes that see a dance where others see rain.

And my son — that small creature who didn't yet know the world — gave those eyes back to me. Gave me back the ability to see things as they are — without the filter of pessimism, without the layer of fear, without the glass of disappointment.

Children also teach us something else: presence. Because a child is always here and now. Doesn't live in the past, doesn't plan the future. Is in this moment — this specific, unique moment that will never repeat. And that presence — that absolute, total presence — is something we adults can learn from them.

Because we live in the past — in regrets, in memories. Or in the future — in plans, fears, expectations. But rarely in the present. And the present — that one moment we truly have — is the only moment in which we can be happy.

FORGIVING YOUR PARENTS

The hardest forgiveness is forgiving your parents. Because parents are the first people who hurt us — not because they wanted to, but because they didn't know how else. Because they were hurt themselves. Because they had their demons, their fears, their unspoken pains that they passed on to us unconsciously, like an inherited disease.

For years I blamed my father for my silence. For not being able to talk about feelings. For closing myself like a safe when someone asks what I feel. For falling silent in arguments instead of speaking. I thought: "It's his fault. He taught me to be like this." And that thought — that simple but powerful thought — gave me a sense of control. Because if it's his fault, it means I'm not responsible. That I can push the weight of my mistakes onto him and keep living, without worrying about change.

But one day — and it was a day I remember because it was raining and the air smelled of damp earth — I called my father. I don't remember what we'd wanted to talk about. But the conversation went in a direction I didn't expect. My father — the same father who never said "I love you" — said: "You know, I regret that I can't talk about feelings. I regret that I can't tell you what I feel. But that doesn't mean I don't feel it."

And then I understood — with full force, with full clarity — that my father wasn't a bad father. He was a father who did what he could. Who gave what he had — and what he had was limited by his own upbringing, his own family, his own chain. And that chain — the same one I'm now trying to break — stretched through generations, passing silence from father to son like a precious gift no one wanted.

From that day I stopped blaming my father. Not because everything was fine — many things weren't. But because I understood that blame changes nothing. Doesn't change the past. Doesn't change me. Doesn't change my father. The only thing that can change anything is forgiveness — and the decision to move forward, with new knowledge, new awareness, new choices.

And that forgiveness — quiet, subtle, almost invisible — was perhaps the greatest act of courage in my life. Because it demanded that I look at my father not as a guilty party, but as a human being — tired, imperfect, full of pain, but loving in his own way. And that love — that quiet love hidden behind deeds and silence — was the only thing my father truly gave me. And it was enough.

THE TEACHERS OF SILENCE

There are people who teach us silence — not because they don't speak, but because their silence says more than any words. They are the teachers of silence — people who, through their presence, their gestures, their way of being, pass on to us something that can't be expressed in words.

For me, such a teacher was my grandfather — the same one I wrote about earlier. Grandfather was a small, quiet, unassuming man. He didn't walk into a room demanding attention. Didn't tell great stories. Didn't give great advice. He just was — and that "being" was so strong, so present, so real that in itself it was a lesson.

I remember one day sitting with him in his garden — the same one where he grew tomatoes and raspberries. Grandfather was silent, I was silent. The sun blazed, the bees buzzed, and we sat in the shade of an old apple tree watching a world that didn't need our words.

After a long while grandfather said: "You know, people think silence is the absence of words. But silence is the words that weren't spoken — because they didn't need to be spoken." And that thought — that simple but deep thought — changed the way I understand silence. Because silence isn't emptiness. It's fullness — the fullness of what can't be said but is felt.

Another teacher of silence was my daughter — that small creature who didn't yet know words, but whose presence taught me more than any book. When I held her in my arms and we walked through the park — the silence between us was like a conversation. We didn't need words to communicate. Her warmth in my arms, her breath on my neck, her quiet grumbling — these were words that didn't need translating.

And those teachers of silence — grandfather, my daughter, and others I've met on my way — taught me that the most important things in life aren't spoken. They're lived. They're felt. They're present — in the warmth of a hand, in the smell of coffee, in the silence that fills a room when someone is near.

A LETTER TO MY CHILD

I'd like to write a letter to my child someday. Not now — because they're not ready yet. But someday, when they grow up, when they start asking questions I have no ready answers for. When they begin to understand that the world isn't as simple as it seems in fairytales.

The letter would be short. No grand words, no declarations, no lecturing. Because I'm in no position to lecture — I'm still learning myself.

I'd write something like: "I don't know how to be a father. Nobody taught me. My father didn't know how to be a father — and his father didn't either. So we'll learn together. We'll make mistakes — I'll make more than you, because I'm older and have more opportunities. But I promise you one thing: I'll try. Every day I'll try to be better than yesterday. Not perfect — better."

And I'd write more: "One day you'll understand that your parents aren't characters from fairytales. They're people — with their fears, their mistakes, their imperfections. And that imperfection isn't something to be ashamed of. It's something that connects us. Because we're all imperfect people trying to live as well as possible — in a world that gives no instructions."

And at the end I'd write: "Don't be afraid of mistakes. Mistakes are proof you're trying. And who doesn't try makes no mistakes — but also achieves nothing. So get on with life. Do things that terrify you. Love people who deserve it. And remember that always, but always, you can come home. Because home — the real, emotional home — is always where someone loves you."

I don't know if I'll ever write that letter. I don't know if my child will read it. But the very act of writing — the very thought of what I'd like to pass on — is already changing me. Because it forces me to think about who I want to be as a father. And that reflection — that quiet, daily reflection — may be the most important thing I can do for my child.

WHAT COMES NEXT

I don't know what comes next. I don't know if I'll write a second volume. I don't know if anyone will read this book. I don't know if my words will touch anyone.

But I know one thing: writing this book changed me. Not because I discovered some great secrets — I didn't. But because it forced me to look at myself — truly look, without blinking, without looking away. And what I saw — that imperfect, chaotic, contradiction-filled person — was more real than anyone I'd ever known.

Because writing is like a mirror — it shows you what you hide even from yourself. And that truth — that raw, painful but liberating truth — is the best gift I can give myself.

So if someone is reading these words and feels they're about them — it isn't a coincidence. Because this book is about every one of us. About our fears, our hopes, our mistakes, our triumphs. About our silences and our words. About our shadows and our light.

And if these words — these simple, imperfect words — help someone feel less alone, then the goal has been reached. Because that's not what writing is about — not fame, not money, not recognition. It's about connection. A bridge between me and you. A silence that becomes shared.

And that shared silence — that silence between author and reader, between one person and another — may be the most beautiful thing literature can give. Because it's proof we aren't alone. That someone else — somewhere out there, in another world — feels the same. And that shared silence — that quiet, subtle, elusive bond — is a bridge that will survive everything.

THE SMALLER THINGS

When I was younger, I thought happiness lay in great things. In a big apartment, an expensive car, a dream job, a perfect relationship. But the longer I live, the more I understand that happiness isn't in great things. It's in small ones.

In morning coffee. In the warm light of a lamp in the evening. In a message from someone who remembers. In a stranger's glance on the street. In the smell of fresh bread. In the sound of rain on the window. In the silence that fills a room when someone is beside you.

These smaller things — these daily, unassuming, almost invisible things — are the true source of happiness. Because nothing depends on them — no risk, no expectations, no disappointments. They just are — as they are. Beautiful in their simplicity. Beautiful in their ordinariness.

And that simplicity — that ordinariness — may be the hardest lesson to absorb. Because the world tells us happiness is complicated. That it demands effort, money, plans. And the truth is that happiness is simple. It's in morning coffee. In the warm light of a lamp. In a message from someone who remembers. And nothing more is needed.

WHAT WE LEAVE BEHIND

When we leave — not in the sense of death, but in the sense of change — we leave traces behind. Not great, not spectacular, not worthy of the chronicles. But ours. And those traces — those quiet, daily, unassuming traces — are the only thing that will remain of us.

I remember the day I changed jobs. The last day in the office, packing, farewells with colleagues. And then one of them — a guy I barely knew, from the neighboring department — came up to me and said: "You know, I remember when you helped me with a project once. Thank you. It changed my life."

I didn't remember that project. I don't remember what I helped him with. But he remembered. And that memory — that one memory, one moment, one sentence — was like light illuminating my life from a completely new side.

Because we leave traces not where we think. Not in great gestures, not in grand declarations. But in small things — in one sentence, in one gesture, in one moment when someone felt they weren't alone. And those small things — those unassuming, daily, almost invisible things — are the only things that truly matter.

THE THINGS I REGRET

I regret the things I didn't do — not the ones I did. Because the ones I did, I can fix. But the ones I didn't do — the missed chances, the unspoken words, the decisions not taken — are like ghosts. They follow me quietly, silently, but always present.

I regret not saying "thank you" to my mother more often. Not thanking her for her meals, her cleaning, her silence in which all her love hid. Because my mother didn't say "I love you" — but her deeds said it for her. And I, instead of listening to her deeds, wanted to hear words. And those words — which never came — are my greatest regret.

I regret not calling my grandfather more often. Not asking him about his life, his youth, his stories that only he knew. Because when he died, those stories died with him — and no one else knows them. And that loss — that quiet, irreversible loss of stories — may be the greatest loss I can bear.

I regret not being a better friend. Not calling more often, not asking "how are you?", not giving time to the people who deserved it. Because time — the most precious resource we have — slips away irreversibly. And there's no chance to get it back.

But these regrets — these painful, irreversible regrets — are part of me. A part that can't be removed, but can be understood. And understanding — as I've written many times — is the first step to freedom. Because regret without understanding is suffering. And regret with understanding is a lesson.

THE SMALL GESTURE THAT CHANGED EVERYTHING

There are gestures that seem insignificant — and change everything. Not because they're big, not because they're spectacular. But because they're real.

I had such a situation. I was in a store — in line, tired, hungry, thoughts elsewhere. Ahead of me stood an elderly woman — small, faded, a bag on her shoulder. When she reached the register, it turned out she was a few złoty short. The woman began rearranging her purchases — putting back the bread, the butter, the milk — looking for a way to pay.

And then — I don't know why, I hadn't planned it, hadn't thought about it — I said: "I'll cover it." And I paid those few złoty. The woman looked at me — and in her eyes I saw something I'll never forget for the rest of my life: gratitude. Not the big, cinematic gratitude. But that quiet, human, real gratitude — the kind that says: "Thank you. Not for those few złoty. But for someone seeing me."

And that gesture — those few złoty, that one second, that one sentence — changed my understanding of goodness. Because goodness doesn't have to be great. It doesn't have to be spectacular. It doesn't have to be planned. It can be as simple as "I'll cover it" in a checkout line. And that simplicity — that ordinariness — is beautiful. Because it's real.

SMALL VICTORIES

I'm not talking about great victories — a promotion at work, a won case, an achieved goal. I'm talking about small victories. The ones no one notices. The ones no one will ever know about. The ones that exist only for you.

A small victory is getting up in the morning when you don't want to get up. Eating breakfast when you have no appetite. Calling someone when fear paralyzes you. Saying "no" when "yes" would be easier. Staying silent when any other word would be more convenient.

These small victories — these daily, unassuming, almost invisible victories — are the foundation of life. Because life isn't made of great moments — it's made of thousands of small moments in which you choose: give up or keep going. And every moment you keep going — even a step, even a centimeter — is a victory.

And those victories — those quiet, daily, unspoken victories — are the only victories that truly matter. Because great victories last a moment. And small victories last a lifetime.

I LOVE YOU

There are three words we say too rarely. And it's a shame, because they may be the most beautiful words any language has to offer: "I love you."

I'm not talking about romantic love — though that counts too. I'm talking about love in general. Love for parents, for children, for friends, for the people around us. Love that doesn't demand reciprocation — that is an act of freedom, not a transaction.

I remember the day I told my father "I love you." For the first time in my life. I was maybe thirty. We stood in the doorway — me on the threshold, him in the hall — and I said those words. I hadn't planned it. Hadn't prepared. They just came out — quietly, timidly, from behind closed doors where they'd been hiding for years.

My father looked at me. For a long moment he said nothing. Then he said: "Me too." And those four words — "me too" — may be the most beautiful words I've ever heard. Because they were real. Sincere. Free of masks, of fears, of the dread of rejection.

And from that day I say "I love you" more often. It isn't always easy — sometimes the words stick in my throat, sometimes courage fails. But every time I say them, I feel something strange: relief. As if I'd lifted a weight I'd carried for years. As if I could finally breathe with a full chest.

CONCLUSION

WHAT I DIDN'T WRITE

There are many things I didn't write in this book. Not because I didn't want to — but because I couldn't. Because there are things that don't fit in words. Feelings too complex, too delicate, too subtle to describe. Moments that were beautiful — but whose beauty lay precisely in remaining unnamed.

I didn't write about all the people I've met. I didn't write about all the pain I've felt. I didn't write about all the moments of happiness I've lived. Because this book — however long — is only a fragment. A fragment of my life, my experiences, my thoughts. The rest — the larger, more important, more beautiful part — remained unwritten. Because you can't write everything. Can't describe everything. Can't say everything.

And maybe that's the best lesson writing gave me: that words have their limit. That there are things beyond words — silences that say more than any sentence. And that this book — despite trying to be honest — is only an echo of what I truly feel. An echo, not a voice. A shadow, not a reality.

But maybe that's enough. Maybe this echo — quiet, timid, imperfect — is enough for someone else to feel they aren't alone. That someone else — somewhere out there, in another apartment, at another desk, in another life — feels the same. And that shared silence — the silence between words, between books, between people — is a bridge connecting us all.

WHAT WE TAKE WITH US

This book doesn't give answers. Doesn't give ready prescriptions, doesn't tell you how to live, doesn't point the right way. Because there is no single right way — only our choices, our experiences, and our ability to draw conclusions from them.

I wrote this book in silence — the same silence that taught me to observe, analyze, and understand. In that silence I became certain that life isn't a problem to solve, but an experience to live. That it's not about avoiding pain, but about walking through it with dignity and wisdom. That it's not about finding answers, but about asking the right questions.

In the first volume I described the shadows — those we cast on ourselves, those others cast on us, and those life casts on us. But shadows don't exist without light. And the light — though it appears rarely in this book — exists. It's in moments of true closeness, in moments of authenticity, in the courage to stop pretending and start being.

If this book taught me anything, it's that no ideal version of ourselves exists. Only the true version exists — full of errors, weaknesses, failures, but also moments of strength, beauty, and deep understanding. And it's precisely that version — the true, imperfect, often painful one — that is the only one worth knowing.

But there's something else this book taught me. Something I didn't expect. I learned that story — the story itself, the very possibility of telling someone your story — is a healing act. Because when you tell your story, you don't only share it with others — you share it with yourself. You draw out of yourself things that were hidden, and give them shape, words, meaning. And then those things — those shadows, those fears, those pains — stop being something that paralyzes you. They become something you can see, understand, and finally — finally — let go.

Silence fills my apartment again. But now it's different than at the beginning. It isn't empty — it's full of what I've learned. And in it, in that new silence, something is being born that might one day become the second volume.

Maybe.

THE LAST PAGE

This book has its last page. Not because I've finished writing — because writing never ends. But because every life has its stages — and this stage, the stage of writing this book, is coming to an end.

On the last page there's no moral. No summary. No great words meant to change the world. Only one sentence I want to say in closing:

Thank you.

Thank you for the time you spent reading these words. Thank you for the attention you gave them. Thank you for the patience with which you bore my mistakes, my repetitions, my imperfections. And thank you for being here — with me — through all these pages.

Because this book would be nothing without you. Without your eyes reading these words. Without your mind processing them. Without your heart — which, I hope, felt something.

And in closing I want to say one thing: don't be afraid. Don't fear life, don't fear pain, don't fear loss. Because all of it — every pain, every loss, every disappointment — is part of the road. And that road, however winding and uneven, is the only one we have.

So go. Walk through the shadow. Walk through the light. Walk through the silence. And remember — always, but always, you can return to this book. To these words. To this silence that lies between them.

Because that silence — that shared, quiet, elusive silence — is our bridge. The bridge between me and you. And that bridge will survive everything — time, fatigue, loneliness, death. Because it's built of words — and words, the real ones, the honest ones, the ones from the heart — are immortal.

WHAT COMES NEXT — VOLUME II

This book has its end — but not its finale. Because a finale would mean a period — and there's no period here. There's rather an ellipsis…

In the second volume I'd like to write about the things I didn't get to describe here. About the relationships I built after loss. About the people who appeared in my life when I least expected them. About the moments when I understood that life — this imperfect, painful, error-filled life — is worth living.

There are also seeds I have left, from which that second volume could grow:

- Breath — inhale and exhale, the simplest thing we do; in the hospital silence by a loved one's bed I understood that in a single breath lies the whole promise of life.
- Morning — every day I say to myself in my thoughts: "Good morning. Thank you for being alive"; because every morning is a new beginning, a promise that something will change.
- The last sip of coffee — an ordinary, cheap coffee from a machine taught me that every moment is unique and will never repeat; the present is all we have.

But that's for another time. For now I stay here — in the silence, in these words, in this book. And I wait. I wait for someone to read these words. For someone to feel what I felt when writing them. For someone to say: "I feel that way too."

Because that's the goal. Not great words, not great theories, not great discoveries. Simply: "I feel that way too." And then I know it was worth writing. Worth being silent. Worth living.

See you in the second volume.

EPILOGUE

When I close this book — when I write these last words — I feel a strange relief. As if I'd lifted a weight I'd carried for years. As if I could finally breathe with a full chest. As if I could finally say: "This is mine. I wrote this. This is real."

This book isn't perfect. It has errors, repetitions, moments where I could have written better, more precisely, more beautifully. But it's mine. And it's real. And that — in my conviction — matters more than perfection.

If you've made it this far — to this last sentence, to this last period — it means you were with me through this whole journey. Through silences and noises, through pains and joys, through losses and rebirths. And I thank you for it. Because this journey — this shared, quiet, elusive journey — is the reason this book came into being.

So I close this book. But I don't close the silence. Because the silence — that silence that is our bridge — continues. And somewhere, in the silence of your apartment, in the silence of your heart, in the silence between your words — I am too. Quiet, present, real.

And in that silence — in that shared, warm, elusive silence — we will meet again.

Bartosz Osiej
